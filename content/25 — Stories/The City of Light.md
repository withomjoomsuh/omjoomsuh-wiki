# The City of Light

*The last story of the set, after [[The Second Sun]] and [[The Third Rome]].*

**Before you read.** This is a single story, and it closes the set. It is late in 2050. India has come through years of unrest around the hundredth year of its independence, and it is stitching itself back together. Katya Zorina from *[[The Third Rome]]*, now seventy, takes the new through-train from Moscow to Delhi, across Central Asia, Afghanistan and Pakistan. Minato from *[[The Second Sun]]*, now thirty-five, flies from Japan with his father's ashes. Neither has heard of the other. Each of them has carried, for most of a life, a death they were never able to mourn. Both come to Kashi, the old name of Varanasi, on the full-moon night of Kartik, when every ghat on the river is lit with lamps. On the road down to Assi Ghat they see each other from fifty metres away. What happens in those fifty metres is the story. It is told by someone who has watched both of them all their lives. The future in it is imagined. The teaching under it comes from the tradition.

## The train

The train was called the *Afanasy Nikitin*, after the merchant from Tver who reached India in the fifteenth century and wrote about everything he saw there. Katya thought he would have liked to travel this way.

She was seventy. She had a window seat facing forward, a flask in her bag with the last of her mother's rye, and a book on her lap that she opened now and then, and closed again to look out of the window. In the inside pocket of her coat, in a small envelope gone soft at the corners, there was a white card with nothing on it but a Moscow telephone number. A man had given it to her across a lunch table in Washington in 2003. She had carried it for forty-seven years, moving it from one coat to the next.

Gennady had died in the winter of 2041, quietly, in a good hospital. Her father had gone two years later at the wheel of the van on the Chernyakhovsk road, with a crate of bottles in the back that he had washed that morning. Her mother had lived to eighty-eight and died in the kitchen with the windmills. Nina was in Berlin with Agnieszka, both of them grey, both still arguing about paintings. Katya had buried all of them properly, with priests and candles and food afterwards, and stood at the front each time in black.

There was one she had not buried. In March 2046 she had opened the army newspaper at her desk in Moscow and read, at the bottom of page four, that General Vadim Yuryevich Tarasov had died after a long illness. It was three lines. She had folded the paper and gone to her next meeting. The funeral was at the military cemetery at Mytishchi, with a widow and two sons and a guard of honour. She did not go. She had no place there. Nobody alive knew that she had any reason to go, and she had made sure of that herself, twenty-two years before. She did not cry. She had not cried at her father's funeral or her mother's either. Her mother used to say that Katya had done all her crying as a baby and kept none back for later.

She had not told Nina why she was going to Kashi. She did not quite know herself. In 2011, in a file about a book, she had read that people go to Kashi to finish things. Since March 2046 she had thought about that sentence more often than she liked.

The train left Moscow in snow. For three days it ran south and east through a country that was bigger than she remembered: the Volga under ice at Samara, the steppe white and then brown, an old woman at a Kazakh halt who sold her apricots through the window and threw in a handful for luck, the blue domes of Tashkent. At Termez it slowed for the bridge over the Amu Darya, the river the old books call the Oxus. The last Soviet general had walked back across this bridge in February 1989, when Katya was eight, and she remembered her father watching it on television with his fork stopped halfway to his mouth. Below her now the river was only a river, the colour of tea with milk, and on the far bank a boy was washing a horse.

Then Afghanistan. Mazar, with its blue shrine and the white pigeons that rise off it all at once. Then the mountains. The train went into them through tunnels so long that the lights came on, and the children in the next compartment sang to keep the dark away, a song in Pashto that everyone in the carriage seemed to know except Katya. Then light again, and a valley of poplars gone yellow, and a man ploughing with two oxen the way men there had always ploughed. Kabul at night was a bowl of lights. At the station a boy got on with pomegranates.

At dawn they came down through the Khyber, with the old forts on the ridges and the sun coming straight up the valley into her face. In Peshawar a family got on with a sleeping grandmother and a bag that smelled of cardamom. At Attock the train crossed the Indus. In Lahore the station was red brick and enormous, and a young man ran along the platform beside her window for a long time, waving at somebody further down, and when the train had nearly left him behind he waved at Katya too, and she waved back.

At Attari, which used to be the border, the train stopped for ten minutes. Out of old habit Katya went to the door at the end of the carriage. In 2022, on another train, in Vilnius, she had stood at a door like this one with a decision she could not make, and found it locked from outside. She tried the handle. It opened. A Sikh boy with a steel kettle was standing on the step. He poured her a glass of tea before she could speak and would not take money. He had learned one word of Russian from the passengers that morning, and he said it to her very seriously. *Gost.* Guest.

In Delhi she changed trains. The station was so full and so loud that she stood still for a minute just to take it in. The departure boards ran in three scripts, and English was the smallest, at the bottom. The trains were for Ayodhya, Madurai, Dwarka, Ujjain, Kashi. Old pilgrim towns. A woman beside her at the board heard her ask about them and told her they were the big cities now.

## Rukhsana

On the fast train to Kashi the seat beside her was taken by a woman of about fifty in a cotton saree, with reading glasses on a chain. She had been on the *Nikitin* too, from Almaty, where she had been selling silk. Her name was Rukhsana. She was from Madanpura, the weavers' quarter of Kashi, and before long she was showing Katya photographs of her brocade on a tablet. Gold thread on deep red. Small mangoes. Cranes.

Her daughter ran the looms now, she said. The girl had done two years at the district skills college and two years beside an old master who was paid by the state to teach her, which was how weaving had always been learned in Madanpura. "Every district has its thing," Rukhsana said. "Ours is silk. Next door it is carpets. Go to any village and somebody is making something that will be in a flat in Kazan next month."

She had sixty people working for her, Muslims and Hindus together. In Kashi people used to keep count of that. Now, she said, they mostly didn't. Her partner was Mishra-ji, who did the dyeing, as his father had done for her father. In the hundredth year, in the bad months, his son and her son had stood at the end of their lane all night. "Somebody came in anyway, from outside," she said. "They burned eleven looms. Mishra-ji's boy still limps." She took off her glasses and cleaned them on her saree for longer than they needed. "People ask if we have healed. We say we are still sewing."

She showed Katya her ledger. Every page began with the same word, written by hand: *Shubh*, auspicious. At the bottom of each month, before any profit was counted, there was a sum for the widows of the lane. Any widow. "In the hundredth year there were many," she said. "Of both kinds."

"In my country," Katya said, "we used to say that vodka has no passport."

"Neither does money," said Rukhsana. "So it needs a conscience. In our books wealth comes second. Dharma first, then wealth. When wealth comes first, people forget each other."

A boy came down the carriage with a kettle and clay cups. Katya held out a note. He looked at it, and at her, with great kindness, and gave her back most of it in coins. The tea was one rupee. Above the door a small screen showed the next station and, beside it, the weight of one rupee in gold, to the milligram. Three rupees to a dollar, Rukhsana said. At the bazaar people said that by 2060 a rupee note would be as good as gold.

Katya held the clay cup in both hands. In the winter of 1992 her father had been paid in sugar.

Further down the carriage there were a great many old people, some travelling alone, some with a son or a daughter beside them holding a small brass pot on their knees. Katya asked about them.

"Some are going to Kashi to die," said Rukhsana, as simply as she had said the price of the tea. "Some are taking somebody to the river. My city is used to it. In Kashi nobody hides a death. You can cry in the street and the man at the tea stall will give you a cup and not ask you anything." She put her glasses back on. "My mother used to say Kashi is the one place where grief is allowed to finish."

## The flight

Minato's father died in October, in the flat above the river in Hiroshima, on a Tuesday afternoon, with Anju holding one hand and Minato the other and the heron standing in the shallows below the window. He was seventy. The care robot stood in the corner the whole time with its hands folded, and when it was over it asked, quite gently, whether anybody would like tea. Nobody did.

Minato did not cry. He had not cried for his mother either, the winter after Mei's wedding. Anju noticed. That evening, folding his father's shirts into a box, she said, "Takeshi-san cried at films. He cried at baseball. You don't cry at anything." Minato said he did not know why. That was not quite true.

When he was eleven, on the night before his family left Tokyo, he had dreamed a whole life in one night. In that life he had been a boy in Hiroshima during the war. On a clear Monday morning in August 1945 there had been a light, and his mother had been at home, and he had searched the burnt city for her for six days and not found her. On the seventh day his father stopped looking, and neither of them said so, and they never spoke of her again. There had been no funeral, because there had been nothing to bury. Minato had woken up in Tokyo with his hand pressed flat on his chest. He had never told anybody. He was thirty-five now, and he still could not think about that woman for long. He did not know her name. In the dream nobody had ever said it, because to him she was only ever Mother. Under every death since, his own mother's and now his father's, there was that one, older than he was, and it took up all the room where the crying should have gone.

It was Anju who said Kashi. She had nursed his father for six years. She had bathed him and argued with him about baseball and taught him three words of Malayalam, which he used on her whenever he wanted to win. "In Kerala we would give him to the sea," she said. "But your father worried. About money, about you, about whether things would hold. For a man like that, Kashi. The river there takes everything, and leaves him nothing to worry about." She taped the box shut. "And you can cry there. Everybody does. Nobody will look at you."

So in November Minato flew from Tokyo to Kashi with his father in a small box of white wood on his knees. He would not put him in the hold.

The woman in the seat beside him had been working since before the doors closed. On one screen a man with a grey beard and a white kandura was speaking fast in Arabic. On the other a column of code scrolled past.

"Sorry," she said, when she saw him looking. "Client. Restaurant in Deira. Four hundred covers a night, cooks from nine countries. I'm building him agents. They take the orders, do the rota, fight with the fish supplier at four in the morning, and tell him over breakfast what went wrong." The model they ran on lived in a data hall in Dubai, because the owner liked his business kept at home. She looked after it from a room above her father's sweet shop in Ayodhya. Her name was Ishita.

When Minato asked how that worked, she laughed. "My grandfather wrote code for a bank in New York, from Bangalore, at night, because it was day there. My father ran computers for half of Europe from Pune. Me, I build little minds for a man in Dubai, from Ayodhya." She shrugged. "Everybody else got old. Somebody has to do the work."

Minato said that a woman from Kerala had bathed his father for six years.

"Of course," said Ishita, as if it was the most ordinary thing in the world. "My cousin teaches English in Fukuoka. My best friend is a nurse in Osaka. There is a temple in Kobe now that does Durga Puja, with fish from the market." She went back to her screen. The man in the kandura had stopped talking and was eating a date.

Minato could not sleep. He watched a Malayalam film dubbed into Japanese, about a family on the backwaters who build a boat together for a race, and lose. It had played in Hiroshima for four months that summer. His father had seen it twice with Anju, who cried at the same place both times and refused to say why. When Minato was small his parents had watched a film on a plane in which a man in a white suit danced with a woman in a red saree in the mountains of Switzerland, and his mother had called it a Bollywood film. He could not remember when he had last heard anyone say the word.

Afterwards he watched the Olympics from the summer before. There were three halls of events now: the sports, the track and field, and the newest, which the programme called the practices. He watched the final of the [[Surya Namaskara|Surya Namaskar]]. Twelve postures, done as slowly as the athletes could bear, in time with the breath, by teams of six. The judges scored how still the breath stayed and how quiet each body became in each posture. Kenya won. Indonesia was second. India, which had expected to win, was third, and was gracious about it. Japan was ninth. The Kenyan captain, a tall woman with a shaved head, stood on the podium with her hands joined at her heart and did not smile until the anthem had ended, and then smiled, and the whole stadium stood up for her.

Minato thought of Anju doing the same twelve postures on the balcony in Hiroshima every morning at six, in her socks, while his father pretended not to watch through the window. He held the white box a little tighter, and somewhere over Myanmar he slept.

## Kashi

They both arrived on the day before the full moon.

The driver who took Minato from the airport had a certificate from the district skills college in a plastic sleeve on the dashboard, and pointed at it twice. He was an electrician as well as a driver, and he pointed out buildings through the windscreen where he had done the wiring himself. Kashi had been a pilgrim town when his father was young. Now it had trams and a medical college and students from Nepal and Kazakhstan learning Sanskrit, and the old lanes by the river were just as they had always been. Nobody wanted to change them.

Katya walked from her guesthouse near the Assi crossing into a city she had imagined all her life. She had first read about it at twenty-two, in Washington, in a footnote. She had read about it again at thirty-one, in a case file from Tomsk, in the Russian translation of a book a prosecutor had wanted banned. She had read about it every year since. None of it had told her about the noise, or the colour of the dust at four in the afternoon, or the way a cow could lie down in the middle of a road full of motorbikes and a whole city would go round her without a word.

In a lane near the river four men came past her with a body on their shoulders, on a frame of green bamboo, wrapped in orange cloth and marigolds. They were half running, and calling out a short line in Hindi all together, over and over. Nobody stopped. A girl selling flowers took a marigold from her basket and laid it on the cloth as the body went by, and went back to her customer. The men went on down towards the burning ghat, where, people told Katya later, the fire has not gone out for as long as anyone can remember. In Moscow the dead went in vans with dark windows, and you were not supposed to see them at all.

She noticed that people here seemed at ease with who they were. The shop signs were in Hindi and Urdu, with a little English underneath, misspelled, and nobody minded. On a rooftop a schoolteacher was taking thirty children through the twelve postures of the Surya Namaskar, counting in Sanskrit. A woman selling clay lamps from a cloth on the ground did her sums on a phone that told her, in her own dialect, what everything weighed in gold.

On the afternoon of the full moon Minato hired a boat at Assi Ghat. The boatman was old, with arms like rope. His son had trained at the district college as a marine engineer and come home to fit the family's boats with solar motors, but the old man rowed anyway. He said the river did not like engines at a time like this.

They went out to the middle of the river as the sun went down behind the city. People were already setting out lamps on the ghats for the night, row upon row of small clay saucers on every step, thousands of them, waiting. A priest in the boat said the words. Minato did not understand them, and it did not matter. He opened the white box and gave his father to the river. The ash lay on the brown water for a moment, grey and soft, and then the river took it, slowly, without any fuss, the way his father would have wanted.

He did not cry. He waited for it, and it did not come.

The priest spoke some English. On the way back to the steps Minato asked him, not quite knowing why, whether the rites could be done for somebody whose body had never been found.

"Of course," said the priest. "It is in the old books. If there is no body, we make one from kusha grass and give that to the fire with all the rites, the same as for anyone. In Kashi we do it every week. For soldiers. For fishermen. For people the floods took." He looked at Minato. "Who is it for?"

"My mother," said Minato. "She died a long time ago."

"Then come to the ghat in the morning," said the priest. "Bring her name. That is all you need to bring."

Minato said nothing. In his jacket pocket there was a paper crane. He had folded it on the plane out of the menu card, the way an old widow called Mrs. Kinoshita had once shown a boy in Hiroshima, in that same dream. He had meant to give it to the river with the ashes. When the moment came, he could not. He kept it in his hand.

The boatman brought him back to the steps. Behind him on the ghat the conches were starting. He did not want to watch the aarti. He wanted to walk. He climbed the steps with the empty box under his arm and the crane in his fingers and turned up the road into the city, against the crowd.

At the top of the same road, by the Assi crossing, Katya had just turned down it towards the river, with her hand on the envelope in her coat.

## Fifty metres

I want you to see that road.

It was the full moon of Kartik, the night when, they say in Kashi, the gods themselves come down to bathe, and the city lights a lamp on every step of the river so that they can find their way. The whole city had come down to Assi to meet them, and the road was too narrow for it, and nobody cared. It ran downhill between old houses painted ochre and pink and a blue you only find in Kashi, with balconies hung with washing and marigolds and strings of small lights, a temple the size of a cupboard set into a wall with a bell that somebody rang every few seconds without looking, and shops spilling out on both sides: brass and silk and plastic buckets, Ganga water in sealed bottles, phone chargers, lassi in clay cups, and a man frying jalebis in a pan as wide as a cartwheel, the syrup going orange in the light. Over all of it hung the smell of marigolds and woodsmoke and incense and ghee, and under it the smell of the river, cool and green and old, which you could smell long before you saw it.

And the people. Village families with the women in their wedding silk and the grandmothers carried in chairs between two grandsons. Students arguing about a film. A wedding band in red and gold, going the wrong way, playing a Tamil film song very loudly on trumpets. A sadhu grey with ash, and another in saffron talking on a phone. A line of pilgrims from Gujarat, all in white, holding hands so as not to lose each other. Three Kazakh girls with Sanskrit grammars under their arms. A woman with a basket of lamps on her head. A boy selling balloons in the shape of fish. A cow, lying in the middle of it all, chewing, with the whole river of people dividing round her and joining again. And at the bottom of the road, enormous, the moon, coming up orange over the far bank of the Ganga, and below it, on the ghat, the first lamps being lit, one, then ten, then a thousand, so that the steps began to glow.

Katya was walking down the road towards the river. Minato was walking up it, away from the river. Between them there were fifty metres of Kashi.

He was looking at nothing in particular. She was looking at the moon. Then, the way it happens sometimes in a crowd, for no reason anybody could give, a gap opened in the people between them, the width of a lane, and through it each looked straight at the other. A grey-haired woman of seventy in a dark coat. A man of thirty-five with an empty white box under his arm and a paper crane in his hand. Their eyes met, and held.

It lasted as long as it took them to walk towards each other. Nobody on that road was counting, and I will not count either.

Minato looked into the grey eyes of a woman he had never seen, and the noise of the road went far away.

He was standing in a kitchen. It was early in the morning and already hot, and the shutters were open onto a lane. He knew that lane. He knew the smell of the room, rice in the pot and the sharp sour smell of pickled plums, and he knew the cloth lying folded on the step, blue with white squares, that his lunch box used to be wrapped in. A small woman was kneeling at the low table with her back to him, her hair tied up in a strip of cloth, pressing a plum into the middle of a box of rice with her thumb. She was humming. He had not seen her since he was eleven, asleep in Tokyo, dreaming. He knew her at once.

She turned round. "You forgot your lunch," she said. "You always forget it."

She wiped her hands on her apron and looked at him properly. She did not seem surprised that he was thirty-five, or that he was standing in her kitchen in a jacket from a shop in Tokyo with an empty white box under his arm. Mothers are not surprised by things like that.

"You looked for me," she said. "Six days. I know. You went past this lane twice and did not know it, because there was nothing left to know it by. You called for me at the river. You called for me at the Red Cross. On the fourth day your father found you, and you were glad, and ashamed of being glad. On the seventh day he stopped, and you let him stop, and you have been sorry about it ever since." She shook her head. "You were right to stop. There was nothing more to find. I was not there any more."

He wanted to ask her whether it had hurt. She answered before he asked, the way she always used to.

"It didn't. I was at the window with your cap in my hand. You had left it on the hook, and I was thinking that you would get a headache in that factory without it. Then everything went white, like opening the shutters onto snow. That was all. I had no time to be afraid. My last thought was about your cap." She smiled at him. "That was a good last thought. Don't make it into a sad one."

Katya, coming down the road, looked into a young man's dark eyes and found herself at a table in Washington in the spring of 2003, with a white cloth and a basket of very bad bread. She knew the restaurant. It was the fourth of the four. Across the table sat a man of thirty-five with a scar through one eyebrow and his hands lying still on the cloth. He was talking about Pushkin's duels, the way he always did, and she was twenty-three and listening the way she had never listened to anybody before or since.

He stopped talking and looked at her. "How are you, Katya?"

He had never asked her anything. That was what she had loved first. Now he was asking, and he waited for the answer.

So she told him. It came out of her the way the door had opened at Attari, all at once, because nobody was holding it shut from the other side. She told him about the drawer in Moscow, and what it had cost. About Gennady in the doorway in his dressing gown, saying he was not a monster, and how that had been true. About the car with the engine running and the four words, and how he had said yes so quickly that she had hated him for it for a year. About a funeral in Moscow where the two of them had stood side by side and talked about the weather. About page four of the army newspaper. About going to her next meeting. About Mytishchi, where she had not gone.

He listened the way her mother used to look at the rye before she bought it, slowly, missing nothing.

"I said yes quickly," he said, "so that you would not have to say a fifth word. It was the last thing I could give you. I am sorry it felt like the other thing." He turned a piece of the bad bread over in his fingers. "I knew it would come to that. I knew at the second lunch, when you talked about your sister. I could see you would give anything for her. I asked you to dance at your wedding anyway."

"You never said anything," said Katya.

"Neither did you," he said. "We were both good at that."

In the wall the little bell rang, and the man at the jalebi pan drew a spiral of batter into the oil, round and round, and it turned gold, and for a moment the road was there again around both of them, loud and bright and smelling of ghee, and then it went quiet again.

"You never cried for me," said Minato's mother. "There was no time. You were hungry, and you had to work, and your father was so quiet. Then there was a wife, and the noodles, and children, and a whole country to feed, and after that there was too much time, and it seemed too late to begin. So you put me in a drawer. You put a lot of things in that drawer." She reached up and laid her hand flat on his chest, over his heart, where once there had been a shirt pocket with a paper crane in it. "And then you dreamed all of it again when you were eleven, and woke up, and carried me all this way without knowing where to put me down."

"I don't know your name," he said. It was the first thing he had said to her. "In the dream nobody ever said it. Today the priest asked me to bring it, and I couldn't, because I don't know it."

She laughed, the way you laugh at a child who has been worrying about something very small. "Kiyo," she said. "It means clear, like water. Tell your priest. Kiyo, from Osaka, who lived in Hiroshima, and liked pickled plums, and worried about her son's cap."

Then he cried. He walked up the road through the crowd with his face wet and his mouth open, crying the way a small boy cries, with no sound at first and then with a great deal of it. Nobody looked at him twice. On that road, on that night, half the people coming up from the river were crying, and the other half were holding somebody who was. An old man in a white dhoti put a hand on his shoulder as he passed, and took it away again.

"There," said his mother. "That's done." She picked up the blue cloth from the step, wrapped the lunch box in it, and tied the knot on top, as she had done every morning. "Now go back down to the river. You walked away from it. Your father is down there. So am I."

"You did not go to Mytishchi," Vadim said to Katya. "Good. It was cold, and my wife does not like strangers." He smiled, and the scar in his eyebrow moved the way it always had. "Do it here instead. This is a better place for it. Here nobody asks who you were to anybody. They only give you tea."

She found that she was crying. Katya Zorina, who had not cried at her father's grave or her mother's, or in the car with the engine running, or at her desk over page four, was walking down a road in India in her dark coat with tears running into her collar, and she did not wipe them away. A woman with a basket of lamps on her head moved aside to let her pass and touched her arm as she went by, lightly, and did not stop.

"There," said Vadim. "That's the first one. The rest will come. You have time now."

When they were close enough to see the colour of each other's eyes, the cow in the middle of the road stood up, slowly, and the crowd parted round her like water round a stone. In that parting I saw each of them take on a little of the other. Katya felt the weight of an empty white box under her arm, and a small woman's hand on her chest, and the sour smell of plums. Minato felt a flask against his ribs, and the cold of a door handle at Attari that opened when he tried it, and a man's still hands on a white tablecloth.

The crowd pressed them together at the last moment, the way a crowd will, shoulder to shoulder, and for one breath they stood so close that each could feel the warmth of the other through her coat and his jacket. The crane was in Katya's hand. The envelope with the card was in Minato's. Neither of them noticed when it happened.

Then they had passed.

## The river

She went on down the road towards the light, still crying a little, easily now, the way rain goes on for a while after a storm has passed. She did not know the young man's name and never would. She knew she had been somewhere with him, and that both of them had put something down there.

At the top of the road, under a street lamp at the Assi crossing, the man stopped and looked at what was in his hand. It was a small envelope, soft at the corners, and inside it a white card with a Moscow telephone number on it and nothing else. He did not know whose it was. He held it for a while. Then he turned round and went back down the road, with the crowd this time, towards the river he had walked away from.

I should tell you who I am. I am the one who was watching. I was there in the assembly hall in Kaliningrad and on the baseball ground in Tokyo, in a kitchen in Hiroshima with the shutters open and in a restaurant in Washington with bad bread, in the shed and the cathedral, on the landing in Amalienau and in a car with the engine running. I was watching through a boy's eyes and a girl's, a son's and a daughter's, a mother's and a lover's. I saw both those lives from the inside, every moment of them, and seeing was all I ever did. And on that road, for the length of fifty metres, the two pairs of eyes I was looking through looked at each other, and I saw that there had only ever been me.

At the bottom of the road the steps of Assi Ghat were shining. The lamps were lit all the way along the river now, on every step of every ghat, from Assi to the far end of the city where the Varuna comes in, millions of small flames in rows, so that the whole curve of Kashi lay along the dark water like a necklace somebody had just taken off and set down. Far along the bank, at the burning ghat, the old fire was going as it always had. On the river a thousand more lamps were floating in leaf boats with marigolds, turning slowly, drifting north with the current. On the platform at the water's edge seven young priests in saffron and gold lifted their great brass lamps, each with a hundred flames, and turned them in slow circles against the night, all seven together, in time with the conches and the bells and the clapping of the crowd on the steps, and the camphor smoke went up white into the moonlight, and the drums came in.

The woman who had come down the road bought a leaf boat with a lamp in it from a girl of about nine. She set the paper crane on it, beside the flame. She did not know who had folded it or for whom. She knelt on the wet bottom step, which was not easy at seventy, and said the words her grandmother used to say over the graves in Smolensk, *vechnaya pamyat*, eternal memory. She said them for a man with a scar through one eyebrow. She found she was saying them for somebody else too, a small woman in an apron whom she had never met. She put the boat on the water and pushed it gently away, and it turned once and went out into the river with the others, and she watched it until it was one light among the million and she could no longer tell which light was hers, and that was all right.

Further along the same steps, the man who had come back down bought a leaf boat from the same girl. He put the envelope on it, beside the flame. He did not know whose number it was, or who had kept it so long. He said the only name he had. "Kiyo." He said it quietly, and then once more, louder, so that the girl looked up. Then he put it on the river, and it went. In the morning he would go to the priest at the burning ghat with his mother's name, and a figure of kusha grass would be given to the old fire with all the rites, a hundred and five years late, and that would be all right too.

Down on the platform the priests raised the lamps one last time, high over their heads, and the whole crowd on the steps shouted at once, *Har Har Mahadev*, a sound that went out across the water and came back from the far bank. And in that sound the woman kneeling on the bottom step and the man standing further along it, the girl with the leaf boats, the old boatman, Rukhsana in her lane and Ishita above her sweet shop, Anju on a balcony in Hiroshima, a grey-haired woman in Berlin with paint on her fingers, a small woman in an apron, and a man with a scar through one eyebrow were all held in that one sound, the living and the dead together, for as long as it lasted.

Kashi, the old name says, means the one that shines. It was shining. I watched it shine, and I was glad.

## Epilogue

*What the story holds underneath, and where the tradition meets it.*

The set began with a boy who falls asleep and lives another life before morning, the old device of Gadhi's story in the *Yoga Vasishtha*. It ends with two people who, in the fifty metres it takes them to pass on a street, finish a grief that each of them has carried for most of a life. The question under all three stories is the same: who is it that lives a life, and who is it that grieves? Below are the threads underneath, named plainly, each with the pages on this wiki where the tradition speaks to it.

**Grief that had no place.** Minato's mother died in the bombing of Hiroshima and was never found. There was no body, no funeral and no time, and the grief went into a drawer for more than a hundred years. Katya loved a man she gave up to protect her sister. When he died she had no right to stand at his grave, and nobody knew she had any reason to. Grief that the world does not see, and gives no place to, does not go away. It waits. Each of them has stood dry-eyed at the funerals of their own family, because an older grief was taking up the room.

**Kashi, the great cremation ground.** The tradition calls Kashi *Mahashmashana*, the great cremation ground. At Manikarnika the fire is said never to go out. It is held that whoever dies in Kashi is given release by Shiva himself, and people have come here to die, and to bring their dead, for as long as there are records. Death is not hidden here. A girl puts a marigold on a passing body and goes back to her customer. The old texts also make room for the dead who were never found. The *Garuda Purana* says that when someone dies far from home and the body is lost, the family makes a figure of kusha grass and gives it to the fire with the full rites. That is the rite waiting for Minato in the morning. The story's claim is a simple one: Kashi is a city where grief is allowed to finish. See [[Moksha]].

**The dead speak.** In the other person's eyes, each of them meets the one they lost, and is told what they most needed to hear. Minato's mother tells him it did not hurt, that he was right to stop looking, and her name. Vadim tells Katya why he said yes so quickly. The story leaves open whether these are ghosts, memories or something else. It shows what happens after: both of them weep in public for the first time, and nobody looks twice. A grief that was never lived stays in the mind as an impression, a [[Samskaras|samskara]], and shapes everything around it. Once it is felt all the way through, it can go.

**Drashta bhava, the attitude of the seer.** *Draṣṭā* means the seer, the one who sees. The *Yoga Sutras* say that when the movements of the mind are stilled, the seer rests in its own nature (*tadā draṣṭuḥ svarūpe 'vasthānam*, 1.3). Patanjali calls the seer pure seeing, which looks out through the mind (2.20), and says that suffering comes from mixing up the seer with what is seen (2.17). The *Brihadaranyaka Upanishad* calls it the unseen seer, the unheard hearer (3.7.23). *Drashta bhava* is the practice of standing in that place: watching thoughts, feelings and events while staying the one who watches. In this lineage it is trained in *antar mouna*, inner silence, and in [[Yoga Nidra]], where the practitioner is asked again and again to be aware that they are aware. The practice leaves room for grief. The one watching is not swept away by it, so the grief has somewhere to happen. On the road to Assi Ghat the narrator turns out to be the seer. It has watched both lives from the inside, the mother and the son, the lover and the woman who gave him up, and when the two pairs of eyes meet, it sees that there was only ever one watcher. See [[Drashta]], [[Yoga Sutras]], [[Chitta]] and [[Mouna]].

**Each mourns the other's dead.** Katya floats a crane she did not fold and says *eternal memory* for a woman she never met. Minato sends a stranger's card down the river and says his mother's name over it. Neither knows what the other was carrying. Both carry it a little way anyway. The tradition would say they can, because the one who grieves in each of them is the same.

**Wealth with a conscience.** Rukhsana writes *Shubh* at the top of every page and pays the widows of the lane before she counts her profit. "In our books wealth comes second." That is the order of the [[Purusharthas]], with [[Artha|artha]] held inside dharma. The India of the story is rich, and the story is careful to show where the riches go.

**Sewing after the burning.** "We are still sewing." The unrest of the hundredth year is left unnamed on purpose. The repair is shown in a lane where a Muslim weaver and a Hindu dyer have worked together for four generations, and where their sons stood together all night. Forgiveness of this kind is slow and shared. See [[Kshama]].

**The whole world as one family.** A Kenyan captain wins the Surya Namaskar. A Malayalam film makes Hiroshima cry. An engineer in Ayodhya feeds a restaurant in Dubai. A Sikh boy pours tea for a Russian guest. A Russian woman says an Orthodox prayer for a Japanese mother on the steps of a Hindu city. At the end, for as long as one shout lasts, everyone is held in the same sound, the living and the dead. That is [[Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam]], the earth as one family, seen from the place of the seer.

**The city of light.** *Kāśī* comes from the Sanskrit root *kaś*, to shine, and the tradition calls it the city that lights up liberation. The set opened with a second sun over Hiroshima, a light that destroyed. It closes with a million lamps on the Ganga and an old fire at the burning ghat, lights by which the living let the dead go.

*These are readings of fictional people. The future in the story is imagined. The teachings belong to the tradition. Nothing here is a treatment.*
