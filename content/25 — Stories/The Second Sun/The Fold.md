# The Fold

*The Crease, Part II. A story in seven panels. The same boy, a new city, another dream.*

**Before you read.** This is the second of three stories in *[[The Second Sun]]*. The next spring, Minato's family moves to Hiroshima. On their first night in the new flat, he dreams again. This time he is a boy in Osaka in 1985, the son of the old noodle maker from the first story, and he grows up to become a banker. His working life follows Japan from the boom of the late 1980s, through the long slump, two great earthquakes and the crash of 2008, to 2026. In this dream he can see what people really think, written in speech balloons above their heads, like the ones in a manga. The balloon above his own head stays empty.

## The first night

We arrived in Hiroshima in the week the cherry trees along the rivers came out. I was eleven, nearly twelve, and my new school started on Monday.

The moving lorry had gone ahead with everything, so we walked from the station with two suitcases and Mei's rucksack, the one with a plastic panda sewn on the back. Halfway there we passed a small car park squeezed between two office blocks. In one corner, behind a low fence, was a round stone rim with a wooden lid on it. A well.

I stopped walking.

My father said it had been there since before the war, and that the city looked after it, and that he did not think anyone remembered why.

I knew that lid. I knew the moss on the north side of the stones. I had stood on that exact spot and dropped two paper birds into the dark. I did not say so.

The flat was on the sixth floor, above the Ōta River. Mei, who had turned six in February, ran to the window and shouted that there really was a river, like we had promised, and a bird standing in it. It was a grey heron, on one leg, in the shallows. I looked at it for a while. It did not look back.

There was no table yet, so we ate convenience-store bento on the floor among the cartons, and Riku complained that his fish had bones in it, which was true. My father opened a carton marked BOOKS (TAKESHI) in thick black marker and lifted out a magazine as fat as a telephone directory, its cover gone soft as cloth. Weekly Shōnen Jump, the New Year issue of 1995. Everybody in his class had that one, he said. The shop by the station sold out before school even started, and he went to three shops.

Mei turned the pages until she came to a boy standing in the rain. Over his head was a speech balloon with nothing in it. She asked why it was empty.

"Sometimes a person has something to say and can't say it," my father said. "So the artist leaves it white, and the reader fills it in."

"Is that allowed?"

"In manga it's practically the rule."

Riku found the remote. Some channel was showing an old drama, a very young Takuya Kimura at a piano with his hair in his eyes. My mother laughed at the screen and said that when it was on, the office ladies used to vanish from the streets on Monday nights, everybody going straight home to watch. My father said he was sixteen then and he watched it too, and we were not to tell anyone at the bank.

Sōta, who was fifteen and knew everything that spring, said a boy in his old class had put his New Year money into bitcoin and doubled it, and could he do the same.

"No," my father said, fast. Then, slower, "Ask me again when you're older." He looked at his phone, at the number that told him how many yen it took to buy one American dollar, and put it face down on the floor.

My own New Year money was still in my pocket, one new 10,000-yen note, so crisp it was almost sharp. The man on it had a moustache and a careful face. Shibusawa Eiichi, my father said, the man who started Japan's first bank. Look after him.

The last carton was my mother's. She took out a small wooden doll with a round painted head, a kokeshi from her grandmother's house in Fukushima, and stood it on the windowsill next to Mei's pink paper crane. The crane's neck had come out bent when Mei folded it. It was straight now. Only I knew why. My mother did not explain the doll to anyone.

At bedtime I asked my father whether he liked working at the bank.

"It's a good bank," he said. Then he went to check that the gas was off, though nothing in the flat was connected yet.

I lay on a futon in a room that did not smell of anybody yet and listened to a train crossing the bridge downstream. I thought about the empty balloon in the old manga, and the well, and my father checking a gas tap that was not connected to anything. The last time I looked at my phone it said 11:12. Then I was asleep, and dreaming.

## Easy money, Osaka, 1985 to 1989

I was ten, and I lived in Osaka, and my name was still Minato. Nobody found any of this strange, so I did not either.

My father was an old man with small shiny burn marks on his fingers from hot oil. Long ago he had made noodles in a shed behind a rented house, and now he had a factory by the Yodo River that made them by the million. My mother, Fumiko, kept the accounts. Anything that was not the accounts she called a waste of time, manga most of all.

I drew manga anyway, in school notebooks, six panels to a page, with a wooden ruler I had taken from my father's desk and never put back. Most pages had a grey heron in one corner. I did not know why. My pencil went there while it waited for the next idea.

One evening that autumn the news showed five men in dark suits at a hotel in New York called the Plaza. They were the money ministers of America, Japan, West Germany, France and Britain, and they had agreed to make the dollar cheaper and the yen dearer.

"Why would we agree to that?" I said.

My father held up a packet of noodles from the bowl on the table. "Say this costs one dollar in America. Americans buy it because it's cheap. If the yen gets stronger, the same packet costs them more. So they buy less of ours."

"That sounds bad for us."

"Yes," my father said. "We agreed anyway."

As he said it, I saw something strange. Above his head was a small white speech balloon, like the ones in my notebooks. There were words inside it, and they were not the words he had said. *We had no choice.*

From that night I saw a balloon over everyone. The mouth said what was polite. The balloon said what was true. I told nobody. When the news finished, the screen went dark, and in the black glass I saw myself holding my chopsticks. There was a balloon over my own head too. It was empty.

The money got cheap after that, and everyone in Osaka seemed to have some. When I was thirteen my father took me with him to a dinner in Kita with men from a bank who wanted to lend him more than he needed. They ate beef that cost more than our television. They laughed at everything my father said. Afterwards, out in the road, there were no taxis. Men in double-breasted suits were standing in the street holding up 10,000-yen notes, waving them at every car that passed.

My father watched them for a while.

"Come on," he said. "We'll walk."

"It's an hour."

"Then it's an hour." He put his hands in his coat pockets. "When grown men wave money at a taxi, Minato, it's time to walk."

So we walked home along the river in the cold, and halfway there he bought us two cups of his own noodles from a machine, and we ate them standing up.

"The man next to you tonight," I said. "The one with the gold watch. He said the factory is worth three times what it was."

"He did."

"Is it?"

"It makes the same noodles it made five years ago." He drank the soup. "If something is worth three times as much and nothing about it has changed, it isn't yours. It's borrowed."

On the last trading day of 1989 the number he checked every night before his bath closed higher than it ever had. That evening he took me up to the factory roof, which he had never done before. It was cold and the river was orange. He saw the notebook sticking out of my coat pocket.

"Show me," he said.

I gave it to him. He turned every page slowly, under the light by the stairs, the way he read the accounts. He stopped at a page of herons.

"Your mother says it's a waste of time," I said.

"Your mother is right about money. She isn't right about everything." He handed it back. "When I was making noodles in that shed, everyone told me to stop. My father-in-law. The bank. The neighbours. Your mother didn't, but she thought it." He looked at the river. "Get a job that lets you sleep. Then keep one thing that keeps you awake. Don't give that one away to anybody."

"What if I can't do both?"

"Then you'll know which one you gave away. That's worse."

The sun was going down over the river. Beside it, going down at the same pace, was a second, smaller one.

"You see it," my father said. It was not a question.

I nodded. I had seen it before, somewhere, before I was even born.

"When I first saw it, it was the colour of weak tea. Your mother told me to leave it alone, it wasn't using anything up." He was quiet for a while. "It's closer now."

Then he said a thing I did not understand. "There's a well in Hiroshima, in a car park, behind a low fence. If you ever have something you can't finish, take it there. Some folds don't get finished here."

"What do folds have to do with anything?"

He went down the stairs to check on the night shift.

## The year the ground moved, Kobe, 1995

The spring I turned nineteen I went to university in Kobe to study economics. My mother approved. Economics had accounts in it. The ruler went with me, at the bottom of my bag.

By then the number on the news had been falling for four years. The bubble had burst, the banks were full of loans that would never be paid back, and my father's factory had never been busier. When people are frightened, they eat cheap noodles.

Kenji lived across the corridor in the student hostel. On my second night he knocked on my door holding a pile of paper as thick as a brick.

"You're the one who draws," he said. "I saw your notebook in the canteen. Look at this and tell me the truth."

It was a whole manga in ballpoint pen, two hundred pages, about a boy pitcher from a fishing town. It was much better than anything I had done. I told him so.

"The truth," he said.

"That is the truth. The pitcher's arm is wrong on page forty."

He snatched it back and looked. "It is wrong on page forty." Then he sat down on my floor and looked at my notebook for an hour without saying anything, and when he gave it back he said, "Your herons are better than your people."

"I know."

"Why?"

"I don't know. I don't have to think about the herons."

He posted his manga to a publisher in Tokyo that winter. In January a letter came back. He read it to me in the corridor in his pyjamas.

"Thank you for your interesting work. Unfortunately at this time." He folded it. "That's it. Unfortunately at this time."

"They were polite."

"They're always polite." He put the letter in a plastic sleeve, and the sleeve in a folder, and the folder in a drawer. "I'll send them another one."

Six days later, in the dark before six, the ground under Kobe jumped. I was thrown off my futon into the bookshelf and lay under a heap of textbooks, oddly calm, listening to the building make noises a building should not make. Kenji pulled me out by the ankle. Outside, a vending machine lay on its face in the road, still lit, still humming. A long piece of the raised expressway was lying on its side, and the old wooden houses in Nagata were burning. So many people died that the school gyms were turned over to the dead.

For weeks we carried water in plastic buckets for an old woman on our street whose house had come down. She had lost her husband. She did not talk about him. Her balloon said *his glasses* over and over, all day.

One evening Kenji sat down beside her on the step with his sketchbook.

"Tell me what he looked like," he said.

She looked at him for a long time. "Why?"

"So you have something."

She told him. He drew. She looked at the first drawing and shook her head.

"The ears were bigger."

He drew them bigger.

"Bigger than that. He was proud of them. He said they kept his glasses on."

Kenji drew them bigger again, and she laughed, the first time I had heard her laugh, and then she cried, and then she told him how her husband held his teacup, with two fingers under it, never by the handle, and Kenji drew that too. A volunteer from Osaka found the glasses in the rubble a week later, not even cracked. Nobody ever found the husband. She kept the drawing in her coat.

In November the two of us took the night bus to Tokyo, because Kenji wanted to be standing in Akihabara at midnight when Windows 95 went on sale. A man on a stepladder counted down the last ten seconds through a megaphone, and the crowd cheered when the doors opened. Kenji paid for his copy with money he had saved for a year at a petrol station.

"What are you going to do with it?" I said.

"I don't know." He held the box against his chest in the crush. "Maybe this is the door."

## The ice age, Tokyo, 1997 to 2007

In my last year at university a stockbroking company called Yamaichi, a hundred years old, announced that it was closing. Its president cried on television in front of a wall of cameras and said his employees were not to blame. The balloon over his head said exactly what his mouth was saying. I had never seen that before. I never saw it again.

Nobody was hiring that year. The newspapers called it the employment ice age. Big companies took new graduates once a year, every April, and if you missed April, there was not really another door.

That winter the Bank of Tokyo-Mitsubishi offered me a job. I rang my mother from the payphone in the hostel, and then I knocked on Kenji's door.

He was drawing. He looked at my face and put the pen down.

"Which one?"

"Tokyo-Mitsubishi."

"That's the big one." He stood up and shook my hand, very formally, like a salaryman. "Your mother will frame the letter."

"She already said she would."

"Good." He sat down again.

"What about you?"

"Two more interviews. Electronics. Insurance." He picked up the pen. "Don't look at me like that. I'll get one."

"And if you don't?"

"Then I'll get one next year."

"There isn't a next year, Kenji. You know that. They take you in April or they don't."

He kept drawing. "Then I'll draw."

He did not get one. He stacked shelves at night in a convenience store in Kobe, and then in a different one. In the winter of 2003 he stopped leaving his room in his parents' flat. There was a word for that too, hikikomori, someone who shuts the door and stays behind it.

I went to see him once, in the spring of 2004. His mother let me in and showed me down the corridor to a sliding door with a paper screen, and went back to the kitchen. I sat on the floor outside the door.

"Kenji. It's Minato."

Nothing for a while. Then, "I know. I heard your shoes. Bank shoes."

"How are you?"

"Fine." A pause. "Did you see the Tigers last night?"

So we talked about baseball. We talked about the Tigers for a long time, the new manager, the young pitcher from Kōchi, whether they could do it again after last year. His voice was the same. If I closed my eyes it was the hostel corridor.

Then he said, "Do you still draw?"

"Sometimes."

"You're lying."

"I'm busy. You don't know what it's like."

"No," he said. "I don't."

I wanted to take it back. I sat there looking at the paper screen. I could see the light of a computer moving behind it.

"What do you do all day?" I said.

"I write things. On a board on the internet, where nobody uses their name. People say what they really think there." I heard him shift. "You'd like it. It's like reading everybody's mind."

"Do you draw?"

"At night. I post them. Somebody in Sapporo says my pitcher's arm is wrong."

"Is it?"

"Page forty," he said, and I heard him laugh behind the door, a short laugh, the old one.

"Kenji. Come out. Come and have a beer. Just one."

The light behind the screen moved. "Not today."

"When?"

"Thank you for coming, Minato." He said it the way his mother would have.

His mother walked me to the lift. "He was happy today," she said, and bowed. Her balloon said, *I don't know how to help him.* The lift came.

I went only that once. I should have gone back.

At the bank I bought American government bonds all day. The Ministry of Finance was buying dollars almost every morning to stop the yen getting too strong, and the dollars had to go somewhere, so they came to desks like mine. I ruled the line under each day's total with my father's ruler.

"What is it you buy?" my mother asked me on the phone.

"American promises. They pay us back later, with a little interest."

"I hope they're good for it."

In the evenings, once the noodle factory's books were done, she had started trading foreign money on her computer, borrowing yen for almost nothing and buying currencies that paid more. There were so many housewives doing it that the traders in London had a name for all of them, Mrs. Watanabe. My mother was good at it.

"Why do you do it?" I asked her.

"Because the bank pays me nothing," she said. "At least somebody pays us."

My father died in 2006, in his sleep, very old, with the factory still running. When I cleared his desk I found the pens, the seal and the reading glasses where they had always been, and a long pale groove in the wood where the ruler used to lie. In twenty years he had never bought another one. I sat at that desk for a long time.

That year a woman from the foreign exchange department, two floors down, stopped by my desk with a file and saw the ruler.

"Is that wood?" she said. "Nobody has a wooden ruler."

"It was my father's."

"Can I hold it?" She ran her thumb along the worn edge. "You've used this a lot."

"Not for what it was meant for."

She looked at me. "What was it meant for?"

Her name was Yuki. She came from Iwaki, in Fukushima, on the coast. We were married the next spring.

## The cheque and the paper, New York, 2008

The autumn I was thirty-three, I was on a six-month posting to the bank's New York office, on a high floor in midtown. I ate the same lunch every day from a cart on Forty-Ninth Street, because it was the one thing I could point at.

In September an American investment bank called Lehman Brothers collapsed, and its people came out onto the pavement carrying their desks in cardboard boxes. Within weeks Morgan Stanley was wobbling too, and my bank agreed to put nine billion dollars into it to keep it standing. The deal was finished over a weekend. The Monday was a holiday in America and, by chance, in Japan too, so the systems that move money between banks were shut. Morgan Stanley could not wait. Our senior people wrote a paper cheque for nine billion dollars and carried it across Manhattan by hand.

My job that day was holding doors. I saw the envelope for about a second. The American who took it said thank you, warmly, several times, and his balloon said *Just this once.* The Japanese director beside me bowed very low. His balloon was so full of writing, line squeezed on top of line, that I could not read a word of it.

That night I walked up Fifth Avenue to the Plaza Hotel, where the five men had met when I was ten, and stood across the road looking at the windows.

A doorman in a long coat crossed over. "You waiting for somebody, sir?"

"I don't know."

"Get somewhere warm, sir," he said, and went back.

In my hotel room I rang Yuki. It was morning in Tokyo.

"Did you carry it?" she said. "The cheque?"

"I held the door."

"That's nearly the same."

"It isn't."

She was quiet for a moment. "Minato. I went to the doctor yesterday."

I sat down on the bed.

"I'm pregnant," she said. "Say something."

"I'm coming home."

"You have three more months."

"I'm coming home," I said, and I meant every word.

That month somebody calling himself Satoshi Nakamoto put out nine pages about money with no bank in the middle of it. I printed them at the office and read them on the plane home, and kept them in my desk drawer under the ruler.

## The wave, Tokyo, 2011 to 2024

Haruto was two, and liked trains more than anything in the world.

On a Friday afternoon in March, a little before three, the building began to sway, long slow swings that would not stop. The earthquake was under the sea off the north-east coast. Less than an hour later, on every screen on the trading floor, black water was moving across the fields with houses in it. Nobody spoke. Over every head in the room the balloons were empty.

The trains stopped. I walked home with the rest of Tokyo, millions of people in office shoes along the main roads, hardly speaking. At a Lawson near Shinagawa the girl at the till was giving away hot water for cup noodles, and people queued for it as quietly as for a train. I got in at four in the morning. Yuki was sitting in front of the television with her coat still on and Haruto asleep against her arm. She had not been able to reach her mother. When I came in she threw the phone at the wall, hard enough to crack the screen. Then she picked it up again and pressed redial until it got light.

The next day the nuclear power station on the Fukushima coast began to explode, one reactor after another. Iwaki was forty kilometres south of it. On the television, men in white suits held counters close to small children.

On the fourth day her mother rang. She was alive, in a school gym, with a blanket and one rice ball a day.

That night Yuki packed a bag.

"There's a bus from Shinjuku in the morning," she said. "Volunteers. They'll take me as far as Iwaki."

"Yuki. The reactors."

"My mother is breathing the same air. She's seventy-four."

"Then let me find a way to bring her here."

"How? There's no petrol. There are no trains." She folded a jumper and pressed it flat. "I'm going."

"Then I'll come with you."

She stopped folding. "And the bank?"

I did not answer straight away. That was the answer, and we both heard it.

"They need everyone this week," I said. "The yen is going mad. If I'm not there…"

"The bank has a thousand of you, Minato. My mother has one daughter."

"Haruto…"

"Haruto stays with you. Your mother can come. I'll ring every night." She zipped the bag. Then she sat down on the bed next to it, and for a moment neither of us said anything. "I'm not angry," she said. "I just thought you would say yes first, and then think about it."

She went in the morning. I took Haruto to the window to wave at the bus he could not see.

Two days later Kenji rang me at the office. I had not heard his voice in seven years.

"Minato. It's Kenji."

"Kenji? Where are you?"

"On a bus. Going north. They want people with shovels." I could hear the bus around him, and people talking. "I came out. I just walked out of the door this morning. My mother cried in the hall."

"You're going up there?"

"Yes. Come. Bring boots. They say the mud is up to your knees."

I looked at the screens in front of me. The yen was moving so fast the numbers had turned red.

"I can't," I said. "Not this week. The bank."

"Right." There was a silence on the line. "Right. Next week, then."

"Next week."

We both knew I would not come. "I'll draw you something," he said, and the line went.

He stayed up there four months. He dug mud out of strangers' houses all day, and in the evenings he drew the people he had dug beside. In June he posted me one of the drawings, an old fisherman holding a cracked teacup with two fingers under it, never by the handle. There was no balloon over the man's head. He did not need one. Everything he felt was on his face.

Yuki came back in October, with her mother, who moved into our spare room and corrected the way I made rice. Yuki and I never talked about the night with the bag. We were kind to each other. It was not quite the same, and we both knew it, and we did not say it.

That spring, from the bank's window, I saw the second sun again, low over Tokyo Bay, nearer to the real one than I had ever seen it.

After that the years went quickly. The exchange in Shibuya where I had left a few of Satoshi's coins went blank one morning, and the coins went with it. A new governor came to the Bank of Japan and printed money as nobody had before, and when that was not enough he brought in a negative interest rate.

"What does that mean?" Haruto asked me at dinner. He was seven.

"Normally a bank pays you a little for keeping your money. Now the banks have to pay the Bank of Japan for keeping theirs."

He thought about it. "Like paying someone to keep your rice in their cupboard."

"Yes."

"Why would anyone agree to that?"

"It's complicated," I said. My balloon said, *Because we always agree.* He could not see it. Yuki's mother looked at me across the table as though she could.

One March morning Japan played America in the final of the World Baseball Classic, and Shohei Ohtani struck out Mike Trout to win it. Haruto was fourteen and shouted at the screen and jumped on the sofa. There was no balloon over his head at all. He was just happy, all the way through.

My mother died the winter after that, with her computer still open on the kitchen table. The next summer the Bank of Japan raised its rates, and on one hot Monday in August the number on the news fell further than it ever had in a single day. I was glad she did not see it.

## The last page, Hiroshima, 2026

In my last year at the bank everyone on the news said the numbers were good. At lunchtime I went up to the roof and looked at the two suns. The second one was almost touching the first now, and I found I could not tell any more which one was real.

I thought about my father on the factory roof. Keep one thing that keeps you awake.

In October I left the bank without telling anybody and took the bullet train to Hiroshima. In my briefcase were a school notebook from a stationery shop in Tokyo Station and my father's wooden ruler. I walked from the station to a car park squeezed between two office blocks. A brown cat with a bent tail was asleep on the wooden lid of the old well in the corner. It opened its eyes, looked at me for a long moment, and jumped down.

I sat on the low fence, opened the notebook on my knee and laid the ruler across the first page. My hand remembered before I did. I ruled the borders of the panels the way I had at ten, pressing hard, and began to draw.

I drew my life in seven panels. Five men at a hotel. A man in the road waving a banknote at a taxi. A highway lying on its side. A young man sitting on a floor outside a closed paper door. A man holding a door for an envelope. A woman getting onto a bus going north. And the last panel, a man of fifty-one sitting on a fence by a well, drawing. In the corner of the page, without deciding to, I drew a grey heron.

Over the man's head I drew a balloon, and then I stopped.

I looked up. My own balloon, the one that had hung over me empty since I was ten, was coming down. It came slowly, rocking a little from side to side, and settled on the page over the drawing of my head, and turned into ink. It was still empty.

I sat with it while the shadows of the office blocks moved across the car park. I tried a few words for it. All of them were too small. The seven pictures already said it. So I left the balloon white.

I tore out the page, lifted the lid of the well, and let it go. I heard it land. Then I dropped the ruler in after it. The cat watched me put the lid back.

Then I took out my phone and rang Kenji.

"I've started drawing again," I said.

He did not say anything for a moment. Then, "About time. Send me the first page."

"I can't. I threw it down a well."

He laughed, the short laugh, the old one. "Then draw another one," he said. "And Minato. Get the arm right."

I woke at ten past six. I looked at my fingers, sure they would be black with ink. They were clean.

There was one sun over the river. I checked twice. The heron had waded further out. On the windowsill Mei's crane and my mother's kokeshi stood side by side.

My father was in the kitchen, sitting on a carton with a cup of instant coffee and the old Jump open on his knee.

"Papa," I said. "Did you ever draw manga?"

He looked at me for a long moment. "How do you know that?"

He leaned over to the carton marked BOOKS (TAKESHI) and took out a school notebook with a soft, worn cover. Six panels to a page, the lines drawn with a ruler. In the corner of almost every page there was a grey heron. Tucked inside the back cover was the ruler itself, wooden, worn pale along one edge.

The back of my neck went cold.

"I wanted to draw manga for a living," my father said. "Then the bank offered me a job, and in those years you didn't say no to a job. It was the right decision."

I looked above his head. There was nothing there, of course. Out here there never was. I found I could read it anyway.

I took the 10,000-yen note out of my pocket and held it out, Shibusawa Eiichi's careful face upwards.

"Can we buy two notebooks today?" I said.

My father looked at the note, then out of the window at the heron, then back at the note. He found a pen in the carton marked KITCHEN, opened his old notebook at a clean page, laid the ruler along the top and drew the first panel. A family eating bento on the floor among cartons, a girl holding a paper crane, a boy at the window. Over the girl's head he drew an empty balloon.

Mei came in rubbing her eyes, looked over his shoulder, and asked why it was empty.

"So you can fill it in," I said.

## Epilogue

*What the story carries underneath, and where the practice meets it.*

Under the bubble, the bonds and the balloons, this is a story about a man who never said what he meant. Below are the patterns underneath, each with the pages on this wiki where the tradition speaks to it.

**The empty balloon.** From the age of ten Minato can read what everyone else means, and above his own head the balloon stays empty: "Whatever I really thought, I could not find the words for it." Japan has two words for this gap, *tatemae* and *honne*, the face shown and the feeling kept. The yoga tradition has two pages on it. [[Satya]], truthfulness, asks that speech and thought line up, and the Gita adds that true speech should not wound. [[Mouna]], silence, is valued when it is chosen. Minato's silence was never chosen. He swallowed it. A small practice: say one true thing a day that you would normally keep back, and say it gently. And sit in *antar mouna*, inner silence, long enough to hear what you actually think.

**Complicity.** He buys the bonds by the billion and rules a line under each day's total. "I had held the jug." On the last page the seven pictures say what he never did: "every time I had said yes and meant something else." Psychologists call this moral injury, the harm a person carries from acts they took part in against their own sense of right. The tradition's word is [[Karma]]. Every act leaves a trace in the one who does it, with or without orders. [[Karma Yoga]] is about how to hold action so that it binds less. The evening review described in [[Offerings|What OMJOOMSUH Offers]] is a practical start: look back at the day as it actually felt, without defending it.

**The life put in a carton.** The old noodle maker hands back his son's notebook without a word. The real father packs his own drawings into a carton marked BOOKS and takes the bank job, because "in those years you didn't say no to a job." The Gita says it is better to do one's own [[Dharma|dharma]] imperfectly than another's well. A [[Sankalpa|sankalpa]], a resolve, can be picked up late. The ruler comes out again when the banker is fifty-one, at the well. If you have a carton of your own, twenty minutes a week with what is in it counts. The essays [[The Self That Must Be Chosen]] and [[The Four Rooms Of Talent]] go further.

**Shutting the door.** Kenji misses the one door into a job and closes the door of his room for eight years. Japan calls it hikikomori. He comes out with a shovel and a sketchbook, digging strangers' houses out of the mud after the tsunami, and drawing the people beside him. The tradition would recognise that way back. [[Seva]], service with the hands, often opens a door that talking cannot. [[Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam]], the world as one family, is the principle against isolation.

**Grief that started before you.** The banker remembers searching for his mother "further back than my own life", and his father's balloon says *We had no choice.* Some patterns arrive in a family before anyone alive can say why. Researchers have reported possible biological traces of trauma in the children of survivors, though the findings are still argued over. Patanjali teaches that [[Samskaras|samskaras]] carry across the gaps of time and place. Naming a pattern you inherited is where working with it begins.

*Nothing here is a treatment. If one of these patterns is close to your own life, speak to a teacher, or to someone trained to help.*
