# The Fold

*The Crease, Part II. A story in seven panels. The same boy, a new city, another dream.*

**Before you read.** This is the second of three stories in *[[The Second Sun]]*. The next spring, Minato's family moves to Hiroshima. On their first night in the new flat, he dreams again. This time he is a boy in Osaka in 1985, the son of the old noodle maker from the first story, and he grows up to become a banker. His working life follows Japan from the boom of the late 1980s, through the long slump, two great earthquakes and the crash of 2008, to 2026. In this dream he can see what people really think, written in speech balloons above their heads, like the ones in a manga. The balloon above his own head stays empty.

## The first night

We arrived in Hiroshima in the week the cherry trees along the rivers came out. I was eleven, nearly twelve, and my new school started on Monday.

The moving lorry had gone ahead with everything, so we walked from the station with two suitcases and Mei's rucksack, the one with a plastic panda sewn on the back. Halfway there we passed a small car park squeezed between two office blocks. In one corner, behind a low fence, was a round stone rim with a wooden lid on it. A well.

I stopped walking.

My father said it had been there since before the war, and that the city looked after it, and that he did not think anyone remembered why.

I knew that lid. I knew the moss on the north side of the stones. I had stood on that exact spot and dropped two paper birds into the dark. I did not say so.

The flat was on the sixth floor, above the Ōta River. Mei, who had turned six in February, ran to the window and shouted that there really was a river, like we had promised, and a bird standing in it. It was a grey heron, on one leg, in the shallows. I looked at it for a while. It did not look back. I took that as a good sign.

There was no table yet, so we ate convenience-store bento on the floor among the cartons, and Riku complained that his fish had bones in it, which was true. My father opened a carton marked BOOKS (TAKESHI) in thick black marker and lifted out a magazine as fat as a telephone directory, its cover gone soft as cloth. Weekly Shōnen Jump, the New Year issue of 1995. Everybody in his class had that one, he said. The shop by the station sold out before school even started, and he went to three shops.

Mei turned the pages until she came to a boy standing in the rain. Over his head was a speech balloon with nothing in it. She asked why it was empty.

"Sometimes a person has something to say and can't say it," my father said. "So the artist leaves it white, and the reader fills it in."

Mei asked if that was allowed. In manga, he said, it was practically the rule.

Riku found the remote. Some channel was showing an old drama, a very young Takuya Kimura at a piano with his hair in his eyes. My mother laughed at the screen and said that when it was on, the office ladies used to vanish from the streets on Monday nights, everybody going straight home to watch. My father said he was sixteen then and he watched it too, and we were not to tell anyone at the bank.

Sōta, who was fifteen and knew everything that spring, said a boy in his old class had put his New Year money into bitcoin and doubled it, and he wanted to know if he could do the same. "No," my father said, fast. Then, slower, "Ask me again when you're older." He looked at his phone, at the small number that told him how many yen it took to buy one American dollar, and put it face down on the floor.

My own New Year money was still in my pocket, one new 10,000-yen note, so crisp it was almost sharp. The man on it had a moustache and a careful face. Shibusawa Eiichi, my father said, the man who started Japan's first bank. Look after him.

The last carton was my mother's. She took out a small wooden doll with a round painted head, a kokeshi from her grandmother's house in Iwaki, in Fukushima, and stood it on the windowsill next to Mei's pink paper crane. The crane's neck had come out bent when Mei folded it. It was straight now. Only I knew why. My mother did not explain the doll to anyone.

At bedtime I asked my father one thing, whether he liked working at the bank.

"It's a good bank," he said. Then he went to check that the gas was off, though nothing in the flat was connected yet.

I lay on a futon in a room that did not smell of anybody yet and listened to a train crossing the bridge downstream. The heron was still there. I got up once to check. I thought about the empty balloon in the old manga, and the well, and my father checking a gas tap that was not connected to anything. The last time I looked at my phone it said 11:12. **Then I was asleep, and dreaming.**

## Easy money, Osaka, 1985 to 1989

I was ten, and I lived in Osaka, and my name was still Minato. Nobody found any of this strange, so I did not either.

My father was an old man with small shiny burn marks on his fingers from hot oil. Long ago he had made noodles in a shed behind a rented house, and now he had a factory out by the Yodo River that made them by the million. My mother, Fumiko, kept the accounts. Anything that was not the accounts she called timepass, manga most of all.

I drew manga anyway, in school notebooks, six panels to a page, with a wooden ruler I had taken from my father's desk and never put back. Most pages had a grey heron in one corner. I did not know why. My pencil went there while it waited for the next idea. Once my father found a notebook left on the stairs, turned every page slowly, and handed it back without a word. He never asked for the ruler.

One evening that autumn the television news showed five men in dark suits at a hotel in New York called the Plaza. They were the money ministers of America, Japan, West Germany, France and Britain, and they had agreed to make the American dollar cheaper and the yen dearer. I asked my father why we would agree to that.

He held up a packet of noodles. "Say this costs one dollar in America. Americans buy it because it's cheap. If the yen gets stronger, the same packet costs them more. So they buy less of ours and more of their own."

I said that sounded bad for us.

"Yes," my father said. "We agreed anyway."

As he said it, I saw something strange. Above his head was a small white speech balloon, like the ones that hold the words in a manga. Inside it were different words. *We had no choice.*

His mouth had said one thing. The balloon said what he really meant. From that night I saw a balloon over everyone. The mouth said what was polite. The balloon said what was true. Only I could see them, and I told nobody. I was ten, but I was not stupid.

When the news finished, the screen went dark, and in the black glass I saw myself holding my chopsticks. There was a balloon over my own head too. It was empty. Whatever I really thought, I could not find the words for it.

To help the factories that sold things abroad, the central bank made borrowing cheap. Cheap money has to go somewhere, and this money went into land and shares. Prices went up, so people bought, so prices went up again. Nobody used the word bubble until it had burst.

By the time I was fourteen, Osaka was shining. Late at night men in double-breasted suits stood in the road in Kita waving 10,000-yen notes at taxis, because there were more passengers than taxis. Somebody worked out that the land under the Imperial Palace in Tokyo was worth more than all the land in California, and people repeated it until it sounded like something to be proud of. When my father heard it on the radio, his balloon said, *Then why does it feel borrowed.*

On the last trading day of the decade the Nikkei, the number my father checked every evening before his bath, closed higher than it ever had. Everyone on the evening news was smiling, and the balloon over every smiling head said the same word. *Higher.* My own balloon was still empty.

That evening my father took me up to the factory roof, which he had never done before. We watched the sun go down over the river, and then go down again. Next to the usual sun was a second, smaller one.

"You see it," my father said. It was not a question.

I nodded. I had seen it before, somewhere, before I was even born.

He said that when he first saw it, it was the colour of weak tea, and my mother had told him to leave it alone because it was not using anything up. Now it was a faded green, the green of an old American dollar bill, and it sat closer to the first sun than I remembered. My father looked at it for a long time. "Look at it now," he said.

Then he said something I did not understand. "There's a well in Hiroshima, in a car park, behind a low fence. If you ever have something you can't finish, take it there. Some folds don't get finished here."

I asked what folding had to do with anything. He went down the stairs to check on the night shift.

## The year the ground moved, Kobe, 1995

The spring I turned nineteen, I went to university in Kobe to study economics. My mother approved. Economics had accounts in it. The ruler went with me, in the bottom of my bag.

By then the number on the evening news had been falling for years. The bubble had burst. Land was now worth far less than the loans the banks had made against it. Many of those loans would never be paid back, but the banks kept them on their books as if nothing had happened. People later called them zombie loans, dead but still walking around. My father's factory had never been busier. When people are frightened, they eat cheap noodles.

Kenji lived across the corridor in the student hostel, and he drew much better than me. He had drawn a whole manga about a boy pitcher, two hundred pages in ballpoint pen, and posted it to a publisher in Tokyo. The publisher wrote back politely and said no. Kenji kept the letter in a plastic sleeve, in a folder, in a drawer.

One winter morning, in the dark before six, the ground under Kobe jumped. I was thrown off my futon into the bookshelf and lay under a heap of textbooks, oddly calm, listening to the building make noises a building should not make. Kenji pulled me out by the ankle. Outside, a vending machine lay on its face in the road, still lit, still humming. In the street a long section of the raised expressway was lying on its side, and the old wooden houses in Nagata were burning. So many people died that the school gyms were turned over to the dead.

For weeks I carried water in plastic buckets for an old woman whose balloon said *my husband's glasses*, over and over and over. In the evenings Kenji sat with her and drew her husband from what she told him, the ears, the way he held his cup, and she corrected each drawing until it was right. A student volunteer from Osaka found the glasses in the rubble, unbroken. Nobody ever found the husband. She kept the drawing.

Two months later, in the volunteers' tent, we watched the news on a small television. In Tokyo, in the morning rush hour, members of a doomsday cult had let nerve gas loose on the subway. People came up the station stairs with their eyes streaming and sat down on the pavement. Over everyone in the tent, and over every face on the screen, the balloons all said the same word. *Why.*

Late that November the two of us took the overnight bus to Tokyo, because Kenji wanted to be standing in Akihabara when a computer program called Windows 95 went on sale at midnight. A man on a stepladder counted down the last ten seconds through a megaphone, and when the doors opened the crowd cheered as though a computer program could fix the bad years. Kenji paid for his copy with money he had saved for a year at a petrol station. His balloon, floating there in the crush, said, *Maybe this is the door.*

## The ice age, Tokyo, 1997 to 2007

In my last year at university, a stockbroking company called Yamaichi Securities, a hundred years old, announced that it was closing. Its president cried on television in front of a wall of cameras and said his employees were not to blame. I watched it in the student canteen. The president's balloon said exactly what his mouth was saying. For once, a man meant every word. I never saw that again.

That spring I graduated and joined the Bank of Tokyo-Mitsubishi, in Tokyo. On my first morning I put the wooden ruler from Osaka in the drawer of my new desk. I did not draw with it any more. I used it to rule lines under columns of figures. I knew I was lucky. Most companies had stopped hiring, and the newspapers called it the employment ice age. Big Japanese companies took new graduates once a year, every April. Miss that door and there was not really another one.

Kenji missed it. He stacked shelves at night in a convenience store in Kobe, and then in a different convenience store. People his age who lived on part-time jobs were called freeters. One winter he stopped leaving his room in his parents' flat. There was a word for that as well, hikikomori, someone who shuts the door and stays behind it. I went to see him once, and we talked through the sliding door for an hour, mostly about baseball. When I left, his mother walked me to the lift. "Thank you for coming," she said. "He was happy today." Her balloon said, *I don't know how to help him.* I went only that once. I should have gone back.

Inside the room, Kenji wrote. On an internet message board called 2channel, people wrote what they really thought, without their names. To me it was like seeing everyone's balloons at once, with no faces underneath. Kenji posted most nights, and now and then he posted a drawing.

At the bank I learned the new rules. The Bank of Japan, which decides how much it costs to borrow money, brought that cost down to zero. You would expect everyone to rush out and borrow. They did not. They were frightened, so they saved, and prices in the shops drifted down a little every year. My mother put off buying a new rice cooker for three years, because it would be cheaper next spring. The whole country was waiting for next spring.

Meanwhile Japan's money was flowing out of the country, and my desk was where it flowed. For more than a year Japan's Ministry of Finance bought American dollars almost every working day, to stop the yen getting stronger and hurting the exporters. The sums grew so large that people at my desk stopped saying them out loud. All those dollars had to be kept somewhere, and they went into American government bonds. When my mother asked what I bought all day, I said American promises, to pay us back later with a little interest. She said she hoped they were good for it. So Japan, which had stopped spending, was lending its savings to America, which never stopped. My desk bought the bonds by the billion, and I ruled the line under each day's total with my father's ruler. In the mornings, before the market opened, I would catch my reflection in the dark screen. The balloon over my head was still empty.

The year my bank merged into a bigger group called Mitsubishi UFJ, and I had to order new name cards, my father died in his sleep, very old, with the factory still running. When I cleared his desk I found the pens, the seal and the reading glasses all where they had always been, and a long pale groove in the wood where the ruler used to lie. In twenty years he had never bought another. I sat at that desk a long time. I think I aged more there than I had since the six days, further back than my own life, when I searched for my mother.

Money was leaving Japan another way too. A trader from London explained it to me once over yakitori under the tracks at Yūrakuchō, drawing on a paper napkin with the wrong end of his chopstick. Borrow yen for almost nothing, change it into dollars, lend the dollars out at five per cent, keep the difference. He called it the carry trade, and he laughed when he said Japan was the cheapest money in the world. I laughed too. I still have the napkin. In the end even Japanese housewives were doing it on home computers once the children had left for school, and foreign traders had one name for all of them, Mrs. Watanabe. My mother, who had kept the noodle factory's accounts for forty years, became one, and she was good at it. While she clicked, her balloon said, *At least somebody pays us.*

## The cheque and the paper, New York, 2008

The autumn I was thirty-three, I was on a six-month posting to the bank's New York office, on a high floor in midtown with a view of other high floors. I ate the same lunch every day from a cart on Forty-Ninth Street, because it was the one thing I could point at. The ruler had come too, wrapped in a shirt at the bottom of my suitcase.

In the middle of September an American investment bank called Lehman Brothers collapsed, and its people came out onto the pavement carrying their desks in cardboard boxes. Within weeks the other big banks were wobbling, Morgan Stanley among them. My bank agreed to put billions of dollars into Morgan Stanley to keep it standing.

The agreement was finished over a weekend. The Monday after was Columbus Day, a holiday in America, and by chance it was a holiday in Japan as well, so the systems that move money between banks were closed. Morgan Stanley could not wait until Tuesday. So our senior people wrote a paper cheque for nine billion dollars and carried it across Manhattan by hand.

My job that day was holding doors. I saw the cheque for about a second, in its envelope. It looked like any other cheque, except for the number. The American who took it said thank you, warmly, several times, and his balloon said, *Just this once.* The Japanese director standing next to me bowed low. His balloon was so full of writing, line squeezed on top of line, that I could not make out a word of it.

That night I walked up Fifth Avenue to the Plaza Hotel, where the five men had met when I was ten. I stood on the pavement across the road and looked up at the lit windows for a long time. A doorman in a long coat asked if I was waiting for somebody. I said I did not know, and walked back to my hotel.

At the end of the month someone calling himself Satoshi Nakamoto sent a short paper to a mailing list for people interested in codes. Nine pages. I printed it at the office and read it twice on the flight home, when the posting ended.

The paper described money made of nothing but numbers, kept on thousands of computers at once, with no bank and no government in the middle. Nobody could print more of it when they felt like it. Its rules set a limit on how many coins could ever be made, and nobody could change it. I saw at once why that mattered. When a government prints more of its money, each note buys a little less, the way milk gets thin if you keep adding water. I had spent ten years watching the yen being watered on purpose. I had held the jug.

A few days into the new year, the first coins were made. Hidden inside them, like a note slipped into a paper crane before the last fold, was a newspaper headline from that morning in London. *Chancellor on brink of second bailout for banks.*

Not long after, Satoshi put up a profile online. He said he was a man living in Japan, born on the fifth of April, 1975. I read it three times. It was my own year. Later, people who liked puzzles noticed something. On the fifth of April, long ago, America had ordered its people to hand in their gold. And 1975 was the first full year they were allowed to own gold again. It looked like a birthday chosen on purpose, by someone who did not trust paper money.

That winter, crossing Marunouchi on my way to work, I saw a balloon floating over the crowd with nobody underneath it. I never saw another like it. It drifted over the bank's tower and out towards the bay. Inside was a long line of numbers in small, calm handwriting, and I could not find where the line began. I thought of Satoshi.

## The wave, Tokyo, 2011 to 2024

By then I had married Yuki, who came from Iwaki, in Fukushima, and worked in the bank's foreign-exchange department, two floors down. We had a son, Haruto, who was two and liked trains more than anything else in the world.

On a Friday afternoon in March, a little before three, I was at my desk when the building began to sway. It went on for minutes, long slow swings, like standing on a ferry. The earthquake had struck under the sea off the north-east coast, the strongest the country had ever measured. Less than an hour later, on every screen on the trading floor, black water was moving across the fields with houses and cars in it. Nobody said anything. The balloons over every head in the room were empty.

The trains stopped that night. I walked home, and so did what looked like the whole of Tokyo, millions of people walking along the main roads in office shoes, hardly speaking. At a Lawson near Shinagawa the girl at the till was giving away hot water for cup noodles, and people queued for it as quietly as for a train. I got in at four in the morning. Yuki was sitting in front of the television with her coat still on and Haruto asleep against her arm. She had not been able to reach her mother. When I came in she threw the phone at the wall, hard enough to crack the screen, and screamed at it, and at me. Then she picked it up again and pressed redial with her thumb until it got light.

The next day came the first explosion at the Fukushima Daiichi nuclear power station, on the coast. Over the next few days three of its reactors melted down. They had been built to an American design. Something you could not see or smell began drifting over the farms and the sea. People were told to stay indoors, and then to leave. On television, men in white protective suits held counters close to small children. In Hiroshima, old people watched without saying much. They had lived with an invisible poison once before.

The sea had taken whole towns. For weeks the television ran lists of names down the side of the screen in small white characters, and the lists did not end. Yuki's mother was alive, in a school gym, with a blanket and one rice ball a day.

In the spring, after ten years behind his door, Kenji came out. He got on a volunteer bus to the coast with a shovel and a sketchbook, dug mud out of strangers' houses all day, and in the evenings drew the people he had been digging beside. Nobody up there asked where he had been. He posted me one of the drawings, an old fisherman holding a cracked teacup, with no balloon above his head at all. He did not need one. Everything he felt was on his face.

That spring, from the bank's window, I saw the green sun again, low over Tokyo Bay, nearer to the real one than I had ever seen it.

After that the years went fast, the way pages go fast at the end of a long manga, when the artist knows how it finishes and wants to get there.

Years before, out of curiosity, I had bought a few of Satoshi's coins for almost nothing and left them at Mt. Gox, an exchange in Shibuya where people bought and sold them. One morning its website was blank. The young man who ran it bowed to a room full of cameras and said most of the coins were gone. Mine went with the rest. I still had the nine printed pages in my desk drawer, under the ruler, and I did not throw them away. I was always better with other people's money than with my own.

A new governor came to the Bank of Japan and began printing money as nobody had before. He hoped prices would rise and the yen would fall. When that was not enough, the bank went further and brought in a negative interest rate. Haruto, who was in school by then, asked me what that meant. "Normally a bank pays you a little for looking after your money," I said. "Now the Bank of Japan makes the other banks pay it for looking after theirs. It's like paying rent to keep your own rice in somebody else's cupboard."

Haruto wanted to know why anyone would agree to that.

"It's complicated," I said. My balloon said, *Because we always agree.* Haruto could not see it.

One March morning Japan played America in the final of the World Baseball Classic, and Shohei Ohtani struck out Mike Trout, the best hitter America had, to win it. I watched with Haruto, who was fourteen and shouted at the screen. From somewhere further back than my own life, I remembered a schoolboy, long before the war, striking out four great Americans in a row and losing the game by one run. This time Japan won. There was no balloon over Haruto's head at all. He was simply happy, all the way through.

A year later the Bank of Japan began to raise the price of its money, a little at a time. On a hot Monday that August, everyone around the world who had borrowed cheap yen rushed to pay it back at once, selling whatever they owned to do it. The number on the evening news fell further in one day than it ever had.

My mother had died the winter before, with her computer still open on the kitchen table. I was glad she did not see it.

## The last page, Hiroshima, 2026

In my last year at the bank, Japan was still the biggest lender to the American government, bigger than any other country, holding a pile of its IOUs so tall that nobody on my floor tried to picture it any more. On the news everyone said the numbers were good. At lunchtime I went up to the roof and looked at the two suns. The green one was almost touching the other now, and I found I could not tell any more which one was the real one.

I thought about my father on the factory roof in Osaka, and the well.

Later in October I left the bank without telling anybody and took the bullet train to Hiroshima. In my briefcase were a school notebook from a stationery shop in Tokyo Station and my father's wooden ruler. I walked from the station to a car park squeezed between two office blocks. A brown cat with a bent tail was asleep on the wooden lid of the old well in the corner. It opened its eyes, looked at me the way a receptionist looks at someone who is late for an appointment, and jumped down.

I sat on the low fence, opened the notebook on my knee and laid the ruler across the first page. My hand remembered before I did. I ruled the borders of the panels the way I had at ten, pressing hard, and began to draw.

I drew my life in seven panels. Five men at a hotel. A man in the road waving a banknote at a taxi. A highway lying on its side. A man crying in front of cameras. An envelope. Black water coming over the fields. And the last panel, a man of fifty-one sitting on a fence by a well, drawing. In the corner of the page, without deciding to, I drew a grey heron.

Over the man's head I drew a balloon, and then I stopped.

I looked up. My own balloon, the one that had hung over me empty since I was ten, was coming down. It came slowly, rocking a little from side to side, and settled on the page over the drawing of my head, and turned into ink. It was still empty.

I sat with it while the shadows of the office blocks moved across the car park. I tried a few words for it. All of them were too small. The seven pictures already said it, every time I had said yes and meant something else. Words would only make it smaller. So I left the balloon white.

I tore out the page, lifted the lid of the well, and let go. I heard it land. It made almost no sound. Then I dropped the ruler in after it. The cat watched me put the lid back.

I woke at ten past six. I looked at my fingers, sure they would be black with ink. They were clean.

There was one sun over the river. I checked twice. The heron had waded further out. On the windowsill Mei's crane and my mother's kokeshi stood side by side, as if they had been talking in the night.

My father was in the kitchen, sitting on a carton with a cup of instant coffee and the old Jump open on his knee.

"Papa," I said. "Did you ever draw manga."

He looked at me for a long moment and asked how I knew that. Then he leaned over to the carton marked BOOKS (TAKESHI) and took out a school notebook with a soft, worn cover. Six panels to a page, the lines drawn with a ruler. In the corner of almost every page there was a grey heron. Tucked inside the back cover was the ruler itself, wooden, worn pale along one edge.

The back of my neck went cold.

"I wanted to draw manga for a living," my father said. "Then the bank offered me a job, and in those years you didn't say no to a job. It was the right decision."

I looked above his head. There was nothing there, of course. Out here there never was. I found I could read it anyway.

I took the 10,000-yen note out of my pocket and held it out, Shibusawa Eiichi's careful face upwards, and asked if we could buy two notebooks that day.

My father looked at the note, then out of the window at the heron, then back at the note. He found a pen in the carton marked KITCHEN, opened his old notebook at a clean page, laid the ruler along the top and drew the first panel. A family eating bento on the floor among cartons, a girl holding a paper crane, a boy at the window. Over the girl's head he drew an empty balloon.

Mei came in rubbing her eyes, looked over his shoulder, and asked why it was empty.

"So you can fill it in," I said.
