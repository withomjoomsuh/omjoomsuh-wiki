# Not One Inch

*The Third Rome, Part II.*

**Before you read.** This is the second of three stories in *[[The Third Rome]]*, and it runs from 2003 to the summer of 2016. Katya studies law in Washington, meets a Russian officer at the embassy, comes home and goes into the service of the state. She marries a rich man who is close to power. Lithuania joins NATO, and Kaliningrad finds itself surrounded. The father stops chasing women, at an Easter service, in front of the whole town. The mother's vodka becomes a real business, with help from Katya that nobody asks about. Nina falls in love with a Polish woman who crosses the border to buy that vodka, and in 2014, the spring of Crimea, she tells her family at the Easter table. This part is loosely shaped on *Anna Karenina*: a correct and cold husband, an officer, a wife who will have to choose.

## I

On the third of July 2005 the city turned seven hundred and fifty years old, and since nobody could agree which city it was, the Germans' or ours, everybody came.

By ten the bridges were packed from rail to rail: schoolchildren with paper flags, veterans with their medals, Germans in sun hats photographing everything, including us, and old ladies from the Baltic quarter who had brought, God knows why, bread and salt. It was hot. The river smelt of diesel and lime flowers. The cathedral on the island, which our fathers had left a burnt shell for fifty years and the Germans had paid to roof again, stood very red against the sky. At its east end, behind a little iron fence, is the tomb of Kant, and before that tomb the leader of our country, the Chancellor of Germany and the President of France were going to lay flowers at eleven.

I was there because my wife had made me come. She said I would regret it if I stayed at home, which was what she said about most things she wanted to do herself, and she was usually right.

"They invited the Germans and the French to the birthday of a German city," said the old woman beside us, very loudly, to nobody, "and not the neighbours." She meant the presidents of Poland and Lithuania, who had not been asked. "Like a wedding where you invite the bride's first husband and not the cousins."

At eleven the cars came. The old woman took hold of my sleeve and stood on her toes.

"Which one is ours?" she said.

"The short one," said my wife.

"They're all short from here," said the old woman, and let go of my sleeve.

Three men walked slowly towards the tomb, the Frenchman a head taller than the other two. Behind them came a row of people in dark suits, and behind those another row, with wires running from their ears into their collars. And behind the second row, quite still, in a grey dress with her hair pulled back so hard that it lifted her eyebrows, stood Katya.

She saw me. She gave a very small nod, the nod a pupil gives a teacher in a corridor during an examination, when talking is forbidden, and then she looked away and did not look at me again.

I will not write what I thought. I am an old man and I wish to die in my own bed.

My wife had seen it too. She said nothing until we were home and the kettle was on. Then she said, "So that is what she does."

"We don't know what she does."

"Pasha. Nobody stands behind the second row by accident."

That evening Katya knocked on our door with a bottle of her mother's rye and a box of chocolates from Moscow, and sat in our kitchen in the same grey dress with her shoes off and her feet tucked under her, as she used to sit on our floor when she was nine and came down to borrow books. She talked for three hours. It was the most she had ever spoken to me. I think she came because that day she had stood behind the second row, and she wanted somebody from before to know who she had been.

Some of what she told us that night she told so exactly that I can set it down as if I had been in the room. I was not, and a chronicler should say so.

## II

She had gone to Washington in the autumn of 2002 to do a year of American law. She called it learning the grammar of the people who write the contracts.

In February, at the embassy's reception for Defenders of the Fatherland Day, in the big white building on Wisconsin Avenue, a man came up to her at the buffet. He was a lieutenant colonel, an assistant military attaché, thirty-five, with a scar through one eyebrow and very still hands. His name was Vadim Yuryevich Tarasov.

"He looked at the room," Katya told us, "the way Mama looks at the rye before she buys it. Slowly. Missing nothing."

He took her to lunch four times and asked her nothing. He talked about Pushkin's duels and about Washington's terrible bread. She told us about the fourth lunch.

It was the middle of March, and everyone in Washington knew the date the war would start. She wore a dark blue wool dress from a department store sale and her grandmother's amber brooch at the collar, the one with a small fly caught inside it, which she wore when she wanted to feel she had come from somewhere. He was there before her. He always was. He stood when she came in and did not sit until she had.

He was telling her that once, at a duel, Pushkin had stood at the barrier eating cherries out of his cap while the other man took aim.

"Why?" she said.

"To show he wasn't afraid. Or because he was hungry. The sources disagree."

She laughed. Then she put down her fork.

"Vadim Yuryevich. This is our fourth lunch. You haven't asked me one question."

"No."

"Men ask me everything in the first ten minutes. Where I'm from, what my father does, whether I'm married."

"And you tell them."

"I tell them something."

"That's why," he said. He broke a piece of bread and looked at it with real disappointment. "If I ask, you'll tell me something. You're a lawyer, you'll make it good, and I'll believe it because I want to. Then one day we'll both know it was only something. I'd rather wait until you tell me without being asked."

"And if I never do?"

"Then I'll know that too."

She felt her face go hot, she told us, and was furious, because she had not blushed since school.

"You know a great deal about me already," she said.

"I know you have a sister you'd walk into a fire for. You told me at the second lunch and didn't notice. I know you hate this war that's coming and you've kept it to yourself in a city where everyone is shouting. I know you're wearing that brooch for a reason." He put the bread down. "That's enough."

When the coffee came he took a card from his inside pocket and laid it on the cloth between their cups. It was white, with nothing on it but a Moscow telephone number.

"When you go home," he said, "if you want work that matters, ring that."

She did not pick it up. "Is this a job you're offering me, or something else?"

He looked at her for a long time, his still hands on the cloth on either side of his cup.

"Both," he said. "You can take one and not the other. I'd understand."

"And if I take both?"

"Then I'd be a very lucky man and a very bad officer."

She put the card in her handbag. He walked her to the metro and shook her hand at the top of the escalator and did not try to kiss her.

That was where she stopped. She smiled and ate a chocolate. My wife looked at me. I did not ask whether she had ever rung the number. I had seen where she was standing that morning. That was my answer.

A week later the war began. She watched it in her rented room with the sound off, because every channel was the same: flags, generals, maps with arrows, and men in studios who could not hide that they were excited.

Ben Henderson had come to see her in January. He was the son of the family in Iowa she had lived with at sixteen, the boy who had taught her to drive on the gravel roads outside Pella. He was a sergeant now, and the week before his division left for Kuwait he drove up from Georgia to take her to dinner. Afterwards she showed him a Russian film on her laptop, the one about Danila, the quiet boy who goes to America with a gun. At the end, where Danila says that strength lies in truth, Ben said, "Good line. So what's the truth?"

"That you're going into Iraq," she told him, "and nobody can stop you. That's the truth. The strength is whoever gets to say it."

"He laughed," she told us. "He always laughed when he didn't understand. He hugged me in the car park like a bear. I'd loved America since I was sixteen, because of that family. That night I understood that America would do whatever it liked, and that the people who paid for it would be boys like Ben. I prayed for him every night he was there. I still pray for him."

My wife noticed the "still" before I did.

## III

She came home in 2004 and went to Moscow, to work, as she put it, in the legal side of things. When the woman from the top flat asked Lyudmila Petrovna which part of the government her daughter worked for, Lyudmila Petrovna said that she did not know. It was the first time I ever heard her admit that she did not know something.

That spring Lithuania was to join NATO. At seven one Sunday morning at the end of March, Konstantin knocked on my door in his leather jacket and said, "Get your coat, Pasha. I want to show you something."

We drove two hours to Sovetsk, where the bridge crosses the Neman to Lithuania, and walked out to the middle of our half and stood at the barrier in the wind.

"In 1990," he said, "when the Germans wanted to be one country again, the American secretary of state told Gorbachev that NATO would not move one inch to the east. Not one inch. Those were his words."

"I remember."

"Poland went in five years ago. Now this." He pointed across the river with his chin. "From tomorrow that's NATO. Count the inches, Pasha."

I said, because I could think of nothing else, that Gorbachev had never got it in writing.

"No," said Konstantin. "He was a gentleman. That was his mistake."

He stood there a long time with his hands in his pockets, looking at the far bank like a man looking at the house of a woman who has married somebody else. Then he said he was hungry, and we drove home.

That night I told my wife. She said, "He isn't talking about NATO, Pasha. He's talking about Lyuda." She was half right, which with her was usual.

## IV

Katya married in September 2006 in the new cathedral on Victory Square, which stands white and gold where Lenin's statue used to, and smells inside of new paint and old incense, a combination I have never smelt anywhere else.

The husband was Gennady Arkadyevich Belov. He was fifty-two. Katya was twenty-six. He had made his money in the nineties in the port and in fertiliser, and he had kept it after 2000, when the rules changed, which was harder, by being correct with the right people. He was a pale, quiet, very polite man with large ears that stood out from his head. I could not look at those ears without thinking of Karenin, and I said so to my wife during the service, and she pinched me hard enough to leave a mark.

The banquet was at Svetlogorsk, the old German resort up the coast, where the pines come to the edge of a high sandy cliff. The restaurant stood on the cliff itself, and while the soup was served the sun went down into the Baltic behind the top table, and the grey sea turned to copper, then dark red, then night. There were three hundred guests, most of them strangers to the Zorins: men from Moscow with wives from Moscow, men who did not say what they did.

Konstantin wore a cream double-breasted suit he had bought in Hamburg years before, for a wedding that never happened. It was a size too small now. He buttoned it anyway and danced with every Moscow wife who would let him. Lyudmila Petrovna sat at the top table in a dark green dress and watched him without any expression, and I saw one of the Moscow wives look at her, then at Konstantin, and decide not to dance with him again.

Then Belov stood and tapped his glass.

"Friends," he said. "Today we were married in a cathedral that stands where Lenin stood. Think of that. Sixty years ago this city was German, and it had a German god. For fifty years after that it had no god at all. Now it has ours again." He waited while they clapped. "Dostoevsky has Shatov say, in *Demons*, that a people is a people only as long as it has its own god, and the day it shares its god with others, it is finished. Look at the West. It has one god now, and its name is choice. They chose and chose until they had chosen themselves into emptiness." He turned the stem of his glass. "Five hundred years ago a monk in Pskov wrote to the grand prince in Moscow. Two Romes have fallen, he wrote. The third stands. And a fourth there shall not be."

He raised his glass. "To the third."

It was the first time I understood what kind of man Katya had married: a man who sold fertiliser, and who believed, or found it useful to believe, that God had chosen his country.

Everybody stood. And Nina, who was twenty-four and had been drinking her mother's rye since the soup, stood too and said in a clear voice that carried down the room, "And the fourth is Berlin."

The room went silent while it decided what it had heard. Then Belov laughed, quietly, politely, and sat down, and the band began to play. But I saw Katya's face. It was turned to her sister with the look of a woman who loves somebody and has already seen what will happen to them.

At about midnight a tall man with a scar through one eyebrow came in late, in uniform, a colonel now. He kissed the bride's hand, congratulated Belov, whom he seemed to know from some ministry, and asked the bride for one dance. They danced once. They did not speak. I watched Katya's face, as fifteen years before I had watched her mother's in the assembly hall, and where her mother's had shown nothing, Katya's showed something. It crossed her face once and was gone.

At two in the morning I went out to smoke one of the cigarettes my wife did not know about. There was a bench below the terrace, in the dark, and I had been sitting there some minutes before I understood that the two women on the terrace above me were the sisters. I should have coughed. I did not.

"Do you know who was in that room?" said Katya. Her voice was low and very even. I learned later it was the voice she used when she was most afraid.

"Men who don't say what they do," said Nina. "Your husband's friends."

"Don't."

"It was a joke, Katya. A man stands up at a wedding and says Europe is dead, and I'm not allowed one joke?"

"Not in that room. Not with those men. Not in this country, not now."

"Or what?"

I heard a lighter click. Katya did not smoke. Nina did.

"Or one day somebody will write your joke down," said Katya, "and put it in a folder, and the folder will go in a drawer, and I'll have to go and get it out. And I don't know yet what that will cost."

"Then don't get it out."

"Don't be stupid."

The sea was loud below the cliff.

"Do you love him?" said Nina.

"I respect him."

"That's what Mama says about Papa."

"Then Mama is a wise woman."

"Mama has been bleeding quietly for twenty years and buying rye," said Nina. "And the colonel?"

The silence went on so long that I thought they had gone in.

"Go to bed, Ninochka," said Katya at last, quite gently. "You're drunk, and you're right, and both of those are dangerous."

I heard the terrace door. I sat on that bench until the cigarette burnt my fingers. When I went back in, my wife said, "You've been smoking," and I said yes, and she said nothing more, which with her was a kindness.

## V

In August 2008 there was a war in Georgia, five days long. Katya was at the dacha on the lagoon on a week's leave, and she sat on the step of the shed where her mother had made the first vodka and listened to the radio without moving. That spring NATO had promised Georgia and Ukraine that one day they could join.

"It was a promise they had no intention of keeping," she said to her father, who was frying fish. "And Georgia believed them."

"So it's the Americans' fault the Georgians shelled their own city?" said Nina from the hammock.

"A child asks for a knife. The grown-up who hands it over answers for what he does with it."

"Georgia isn't a child."

"Then why does it need a nanny with tanks?"

"Fish," said Konstantin, and put it on the table, and that was the end of it, because his fish was very good. Arguments in that family always ended with food.

## VI

And now Easter.

In the spring of 2009 Konstantin had been seeing a woman in Zelenogradsk for nearly two years. Her name was Alla, she had a hair salon by the promenade where the cats sleep on the benches, and everybody knew. I knew. The woman from the top flat knew. Taras Ivanovich knew, and once in the courtyard he said something to Konstantin in Ukrainian that made him go white and walk away. Lyudmila Petrovna knew. She said nothing. She bought rye.

On Holy Saturday Konstantin went to Zelenogradsk in the afternoon and did not come back for supper. Lyudmila Petrovna baked her kulich, painted her eggs with onion skins, and made her paskha in the wooden mould that had been her grandmother's, and at eleven she put on her white scarf and walked to the cathedral on Victory Square with Nina, my wife and me. Katya was in Moscow.

By half past eleven the cathedral was so full that people stood out on the steps and down into the square, each with a thin candle, unlit, the old women in front because the old women are always in front, the young men at the back because they are always at the back, children asleep on shoulders, soldiers from the garrison in a block of grey near the doors, and over all of it the smell of beeswax and wet stone and the cheap sweet incense the priests buy by the kilo. At midnight the priests came out with their candles lit, and the light was passed from candle to candle through the whole crowd, out of the doors, down the steps, until the square was full of small flames shaking in the wind off the river. The priest cried, "Christ is risen!" and three thousand people answered, "He is risen indeed!" and he cried it again, and again, and the bells began.

Konstantin came in at about that moment. I saw him at the back by the doors, among the soldiers, in his good coat, with no candle. He had driven from Zelenogradsk. Nobody knows what happened there that evening. He has never said.

Lyudmila Petrovna saw him too. She did not hesitate. She turned round and walked the whole length of the cathedral, against the crowd, with her candle held up in front of her so that it would not go out, and people let her through without knowing why. She reached him by the doors and kissed him three times, right cheek, left, right, the Easter kiss, and said what you say. "Christ is risen."

You must answer, "He is risen indeed." A child of three knows it. A drunk at a bus stop knows it. Konstantin Sergeyevich opened his mouth and could not say it. He stood among the soldiers with his mouth open and the tears running into his moustache, and she waited, holding her candle, with the bells ringing over both of them. At last he lit his own candle from hers, because he did not know what else to do with his hands, and the woman from the top flat, who was standing near my wife, said, "Well. There."

The next afternoon he drove to Zelenogradsk and came back in an hour, and went to the shed where Lyudmila kept the empty bottles and asked her how she wanted them washed.

He has washed them ever since. He drives the van. He does the accounts in a school exercise book in very small figures. He sits beside her at the market on Saturdays and talks to the customers, because he is charming and she is not, and from that day, as far as anyone knows, he has not looked at another woman.

A year later, on the stairs, I asked him what had happened that night. It was the only time I asked.

"Pasha," he said, "I lied to everyone for twenty years and it cost me nothing. That night, standing there, I found out I'd been lying to myself, and I couldn't bear it. It was like finding out you've been walking around for twenty years with your trousers down."

## VII

In 2010 the vodka became legal.

In our country you cannot simply make vodka and sell it, however good it is. You need a factory and a great many papers, and the papers take years. Lyudmila Petrovna had sold her rye for eighteen years to people who came to the door, to half the officers of the fleet, and to a deputy of the regional parliament who sent his driver, and everyone had looked the other way, because everyone was drinking it. In the spring of 2010 an inspector came. He was young and he did not drink. He wrote a great many things in a folder.

Lyudmila Petrovna rang Katya that evening. I was in her kitchen eating pickled mushrooms. She spoke with her back to me for perhaps two minutes and said almost nothing. "Yes. Yes. A young one. No, I didn't. Yes. Thank you, daughter."

A month later she had a contract with a licensed plant on the Chernyakhovsk road, where her rye would be made under her own eye in her grandmother's way and bottled under a plain white label that said only ZORINA. Nobody heard of the inspector's folder again. Other people in the region had waited four years for such a contract.

I asked her once whether she had asked Katya what she had done. She was rolling dough and did not look up.

"I didn't ask her," she said. "She didn't tell me. We are a family of people who don't tell each other things, Pavel Andreyevich, and we love each other terribly."

My wife died that winter, a week before the New Year, of the thing in her lungs that she had known about since the summer and told nobody, not even me. I will say no more than that, except that for forty days Lyudmila Petrovna came down every evening with soup, and set it on the table, and never once asked me how I was, which was the only thing I could have borne.

## VIII

In the summer of 2012 the Poles began to come. A new permit let people on both sides of the border cross without a visa, and for four years the border was something you crossed for groceries: the Poles came for our petrol and vodka, and we went for their cheese. My wife would have said it was the most normal thing that had happened in her lifetime.

Lyudmila Petrovna had a stall at the Central Market by then, with Konstantin beside her talking, and Nina helped on Saturdays. Nina was thirty, a curator at the art gallery, still unmarried, which the woman from the top flat mentioned to me about once a month.

In the spring of 2013 a Polish woman began to come to the stall every four weeks and buy six bottles. She was tall, with short hair the colour of rye before it is cut, and paint under her fingernails. Her name was Agnieszka Wróbel. She restored paintings at a museum in Gdańsk, and she bought the six bottles, she said in careful Russian, for her father, who said that Russians could do only two things well, and this was one of them.

"And the other?" asked Konstantin, delighted.

"Suffer beautifully," said Agnieszka, and Konstantin laughed so hard he had to sit down, and Lyudmila Petrovna, who never laughed at the market, laughed too.

Nina did not laugh. I was there, buying mushrooms, and I saw Nina look at her, and go on looking after Agnieszka had paid and gone, until her father asked whether she had been turned to salt.

Agnieszka came every four weeks after that. Then every two. Then she came on days when she bought nothing.

## IX

In 2014 Easter fell on the same day for us and the Catholics, and Lyudmila Petrovna said it was a sign and that Nina should bring her Polish friend from the museum to the Easter table. So she did.

It was five weeks after Crimea, and I must say honestly that almost everybody I knew was happy that spring, the way people are happy when an old injury stops hurting. The children had painted the two words on the courtyard wall in letters a metre high. Konstantin had a small flag of the Black Sea Fleet in the van window. Taras Ivanovich, whose sister lived in Poltava, had stopped coming to the market for a month, then started again, and nobody mentioned it.

Katya came from Moscow. Belov did not. There were eight of us round the table in the kitchen with the windmills: the parents, the two daughters, Agnieszka, Taras, the woman from the top flat and I.

Konstantin raised a glass to Crimea.

Agnieszka put her glass down. She did it politely and said nothing.

Katya noticed. Katya noticed everything. She spoke to Agnieszka in a perfectly friendly voice. "You think we stole it."

"I think," said Agnieszka slowly, "that in my country we know what it is when a big neighbour decides which parts of you are really his."

"In December," said Katya, "an American minister walked round the Maidan in Kyiv handing out buns. Then we all heard her on the telephone deciding who would run Ukraine. Then the elected president ran for his life. You can call that a revolution. I call it somebody else's hand in the pot."

"A hundred people were shot on the Maidan," said Nina. "Who chose them, Katya?"

"It is being investigated."

"By whom?"

Taras put his hand flat on the table. "My sister in Poltava," he said, "says the Russians are coming. My nephew here says the Russians are already here and they are us. I have lived in this city forty-three years. I don't know who I am this spring. Lyuda, pour." She poured.

Then Nina reached over and put her hand on Agnieszka's hand, on the tablecloth between the kulich and the salt, and left it there.

"Mama," she said. "She isn't my friend from the museum."

The year before, parliament had passed its law against what it called propaganda of non-traditional relations, and the woman from the top flat was sitting at the table. It was so quiet I could hear the tap dripping.

Konstantin spoke first. He looked at the two hands for a long time, then at his own. "I have loved a great many women badly," he said. "If you can love one well, you are a better person than your father." He drank, alone, and wiped his moustache.

Lyudmila Petrovna said, "I knew when you were nine. Eat."

The woman from the top flat ate. Taras crossed himself and ate. Katya said nothing at all. She looked at her sister's hand on the Polish woman's hand, and up at the icon in the corner, and then she cut a piece of kulich, passed the plate to Agnieszka, and said, "Try the paskha. Mama's is the best in the city."

At midnight I went down to my flat. I had left my door open to let out the smoke, because Taras had been smoking on the stairs, and I was taking off my shoes when I heard the sisters on the steps above me.

"Are you going to report me?" said Nina.

"Don't be stupid."

"It's your job, isn't it? Reading laws. That's a law."

"My job is to read the laws," said Katya. "Yours is to stay out of them. That was all I asked."

"You never ask. You just arrange."

"Somebody has to."

"I'm going to live with her."

"Not here."

"Then where?"

The light on the landing went out. It is on a timer. Neither of them pressed it again.

"Somewhere I can still reach you," said Katya in the dark. "Gdańsk. Berlin. Not Moscow and not here. Somewhere I can still reach."

"And if you can't?"

"I can always reach."

"That's what frightens me," said Nina.

I heard someone sit down on the stairs, and then a sound I did not recognise at first, because I had never heard it from that family. One of them was crying, and it was not Nina.

After a while Katya came down past my open door and saw me in the hall with one shoe in my hand. She stopped on the step above me, so that our heads were level, and her face was perfectly dry.

"Pavel Andreyevich," she said. "If anybody ever asks you, Agnieszka is Nina's colleague from the museum in Gdańsk."

"And if nobody asks?"

"Then you have never seen her." She kissed me on the forehead, which she had not done since she was eleven, and went back up.

## X

And now the thing I am least proud of, because I saw it by accident and have never told anyone.

In June 2016 I went to Svetlogorsk for a week for my chest. On the third morning, walking early on the upper promenade, I passed the small grey hotel the Moscow people use, the one with no sign. A black car with Moscow plates was waiting outside. A tall man came out of the door in civilian clothes, with a scar through one eyebrow. He was a general now. I had seen his photograph in the paper. He got into the car and it drove away. I sat down on a bench with my newspaper. Ten minutes later Katya came out of the same door in a raincoat, with her hair wet, and walked straight along the promenade towards me, and stopped.

"Good morning, Pavel Andreyevich," she said. "The sea air is good for the chest." And she walked on.

She did not ask me to keep quiet. She knew she had no need to.

I have taught *Anna Karenina* eleven times. That morning, out of breath on a bench, I thought of Anna at the last station, deciding alone what she owed and to whom. And I thought: Katya will not throw herself under a train. She is too much a lawyer.

Three weeks later the Poles closed the border arrangement. From that morning Agnieszka needed a visa to come to the market, and Nina needed one to go to Gdańsk. Nina drove to the crossing at Mamonovo that day and sat in the car on our side, watching the Polish lorries go through as usual, and laughed, she told me, because the only border they had closed was the one for people buying cheese.

That evening, by chance, both sisters were in their mother's kitchen. Lyudmila Petrovna put the kettle on and the rye out, and the three of them sat under the cracked windmill without speaking, and I sat with them, because I had been passing and she had called me in.

After a long time Lyudmila Petrovna said, "Tolstoy says all happy families are alike." She turned her glass round on the table. "I've never met one. Have you, Pavel Andreyevich? You've read more than me."

I said I had not.

"Then he was guessing," she said, and poured.

## Epilogue

*What the story holds underneath, and where the tradition meets it.*

Part II is about what a family does with its failures: a husband's, a state's, its own. Below are the patterns underneath, named plainly, each with the pages on this wiki where the tradition speaks to it.

**Desire with no edges.** Konstantin's twenty years of women, counted by nobody. The tradition places [[Kama|kama]] third among the aims of life, after dharma and artha, so that desire has a boundary before it begins. Without one it becomes the first of the [[Shadripus|shadripus]], the six enemies of the mind, and it scatters a man until he cannot answer three words at Easter.

**The lie told to oneself.** "I lied to everyone for twenty years and it was nothing. And on that night I found out that I had been lying to myself, and it was unbearable." Zosima says it first: above all, do not lie to yourself. [[Satya]] starts with what a person will admit in private. The rest follows from there.

**Service after a fall.** For eighteen years Konstantin washes bottles, drives the van and keeps the accounts in very small figures. He asks nothing for it. The tradition calls this [[Seva|seva]], and the Gita calls the attitude behind it [[Karma Yoga|karma yoga]]: work done for someone else, with no eye on the reward. It is how he repairs what he broke.

**The favour.** One phone call, and a licence that takes others four years arrives in a month. Lyudmila does not ask how. "We are a family of people who don't tell each other things." This is [[Artha|artha]], wealth, bending dharma for the family's gain. The Gita warns against wealth gathered by crooked means (Bhagavad Gita 16.12). The story does not punish the favour. It asks the reader to notice it.

**A place at the table.** Nina puts her hand on Agnieszka's, between the kulich and the salt. Her father says she loves better than he did. Her mother says, "I knew when you were nine. Eat." In 2014, with a new law against it on the books, the family keeps its daughter. That is kuldharma, the second of the [[Four Dharmas]]. Katya's quiet word on the stairs is the same duty, done from inside the state.

**The officer and the vow.** Katya dances once with Vadim at her own wedding, and something crosses her face. Ten years later she walks out of a hotel in Svetlogorsk and greets her old teacher as if nothing were strange. Kama and kuldharma now pull in opposite directions inside one woman. Part III shows which one she keeps, and what it costs.
