# Not One Inch

*The Third Rome, Part II.*

**Before you read.** This is the second of three stories in *[[The Third Rome]]*, and it runs from 2003 to the summer of 2016. Katya studies law in Washington, meets a Russian officer at the embassy, comes home and goes into the service of the state. She marries a rich man who is close to power. Lithuania joins NATO, and Kaliningrad finds itself surrounded. The father stops chasing women, at an Easter service, in front of the whole town. The mother's vodka becomes a real business, with help from Katya that nobody asks about. Nina falls in love with a Polish woman who crosses the border to buy that vodka, and in 2014, the spring of Crimea, she tells her family at the Easter table. This part is loosely shaped on *Anna Karenina*: a correct and cold husband, an officer, a wife who will have to choose.

## I

On the third of July 2005 the city turned seven hundred and fifty years old, and since nobody could agree which city it was, the Germans' or ours, everybody came.

The island in the river where the cathedral stands had been swept for a week. The linden walks had been planted with petunias in the colours of the flag, and on the morning of the jubilee the petunias were found to be the colours of the wrong flag in one bed, the French, which was blamed on the gardeners and quietly fixed before nine. By ten the bridges were packed from rail to rail with people who had been told to come and people who had come anyway, schoolchildren with paper flags, veterans with their medals and their folding stools, Germans in sun hats taking photographs of everything, including us, women from the market who had closed their stalls and brought sunflower seeds, young men from the fleet in white, and the old ladies from the Baltic quarter who had come in their best scarves to see the leader walk past, and who had brought, God knows why, bread and salt. It was hot. The river smelt of diesel and lime flowers. Above the crowd the cathedral, which our fathers had left as a burnt shell for fifty years and which the Germans had paid to give a roof again, stood very red against a sky that seemed to have been washed for the occasion. And at the east end of it, behind a little iron fence, was the tomb of Kant, a plain stone porch with columns, before which the leader of our country and the Chancellor of Germany were going to lay flowers at eleven.

I was there with a paper flag that a child had given me. I do not know why I kept holding it.

"They invited the Germans to the birthday of a German city," said the old woman beside me, very loudly, to nobody, "and not the neighbours." She meant the presidents of Poland and Lithuania, who had not been asked, and about whom the whole city had been gossiping for a month. "Ha. Like a wedding where you invite the bride's first husband and not the cousins."

At eleven they came. A movement went through the crowd like wind through rye. I saw the two men, the short one and the tall one, walking together slowly towards the tomb, and behind them a few people in dark suits, and behind those another row of people in dark suits, with wires coming from their ears into their collars. And behind the second row, quite still, in a grey dress with her hair pulled back so hard that it pulled her eyebrows up, stood Katya.

She saw me. She made a very small movement of the head, the movement a pupil makes to a teacher in a corridor during an examination, and then she looked away and did not look at me again.

I will not write what I thought. I am an old man and I wish to die in my own bed.

That evening she knocked on my door with a bottle of her mother's rye and a box of chocolates from Moscow, and sat in my kitchen in the same grey dress with her shoes off, and talked for three hours. It was the most she had ever said to me, and I think now that she came to say it because that day she had stood behind the second row, and she wanted somebody from before to know who she had been.

## II

She had gone to Washington in the autumn of 2002, after her degree here, to do a year of American law at a university there. The Americans called it a master's. She called it "learning the grammar of the people who write the contracts."

In February, at the reception the embassy gives every year on the twenty-third, for Defenders of the Fatherland Day, in the big white building on Wisconsin Avenue, a man came up to her by the buffet. He was a lieutenant colonel, an assistant military attaché, thirty-five, with a scar through one eyebrow and very still hands. His name was Vadim Yuryevich Tarasov.

"He looked at the room," Katya told me, "the way Mama looks at the rye before she buys it."

He took her to lunch four times, in four different places, and he asked her nothing. Nothing about her studies, her family, her opinions, nothing. He talked about Pushkin's duels and about Washington's terrible bread. "Men usually ask me everything," she said. "I found it very interesting, a man who asked nothing." The fifth time he gave her a card with nothing on it but a Moscow telephone number, and said that when she came home, if she ever wanted work that mattered, she might ring it.

She told me that much and stopped, and smiled, and ate a chocolate. I did not ask whether she ever rang it. A teacher learns which questions will cost a pupil.

In March the Americans went into Iraq. She watched it on the television in her rented room in Georgetown with the sound off, she said, because the sound was all flags. "They promised Gorbachev not one inch," she said in my kitchen, "and here they were, going seven thousand miles, wherever they liked, with nobody in the world able to say no."

Ben Henderson had come to see her in January, a week before he left for Kuwait with his division. He was a sergeant by then, and he drove up from his base in Georgia in a car full of energy-drink cans to take her to dinner. She showed him a film afterwards on her laptop, the Russian film about Danila, the quiet boy who goes to America with a gun and a Walkman, and at the end of it, where Danila asks the American where strength lies and answers himself that strength is in truth, Ben said, "That's a good line. So what's the truth?"

"That you are going into Iraq," she told him, "and nobody can stop you. That is the truth. The strength is whoever gets to say it."

"He laughed," she told me. "He always laughed when he didn't understand. He was the kindest man I ever met. He hugged me in the car park like a bear. I prayed for him every night he was in Iraq. I still pray for him."

I remember that she said "still."

## III

She came home in 2004 and went to Moscow, to work, as she put it, "in the legal side of things." Her parents told the neighbours she was a lawyer for the government. When the woman from the top flat asked which part of the government, Lyudmila Petrovna said, "The part with lawyers," and closed the door.

That was the spring that Lithuania joined NATO, on the twenty-ninth of March 2004, together with Latvia and Estonia and four others. From that morning, if you drove out of Kaliningrad in any direction except into the sea, you arrived in NATO. The old women in the courtyard said that now we were an island. We had been an island since 1991.

Konstantin took it hard. He drove the Audi to the Lithuanian border at Sovetsk one Sunday and stood on our side of the bridge over the Neman and looked across at the other side for an hour, Katya told me, "like a man looking at the house of a woman who has married someone else."

## IV

She married in September 2006, two weeks after the new cathedral on Victory Square was consecrated, and she was one of the first brides in it.

The cathedral deserves a sentence. It stands on the square where Lenin used to, white, with gold domes, and inside it is enormous and smells of new paint and old incense, a combination I have never smelt anywhere else.

The husband was Gennady Arkadyevich Belov. He was fifty-two. Katya was twenty-six. He had made his money in the nineties, in the port and in fertiliser, which comes through the port, and he had kept it in the two-thousands, which is the more difficult thing, by being correct with the right people. He was a pale, quiet, very polite man, with large ears that stood out from his head, and I could not look at those ears without thinking of Karenin, and I am ashamed to say that I said so to my wife at the wedding, and she pinched me.

The banquet was at a restaurant on the cliff at Svetlogorsk, with the Baltic going pink below the windows. There were three hundred guests, most of them strangers to the Zorins, men from Moscow with wives from Moscow, men who did not say what they did. Konstantin was magnificent in a suit from Hamburg and danced with every Moscow wife who would let him. Lyudmila Petrovna sat at the top table in a dark green dress and watched him without any expression, and I saw a Moscow wife look at her, and look at Konstantin, and decide not to dance with him again.

Belov made a toast. It was a long toast, and it was learned, and it was the first time I understood what kind of man Katya had married. He spoke of the cathedral where they had been married, and of the city that had been German and was now Russian, and then he quoted Dostoevsky, from *Demons*, the words of Shatov: that a people is a people only so long as it has its own god, and that the moment a people has a common god with others, it is finished. "The West has one god now," he said, "and its name is choice. The choice of the customer. They chose themselves into emptiness." And then he quoted the monk from Pskov, Philotheus, who wrote to the grand prince five hundred years ago: two Romes have fallen, and the third stands, and a fourth there shall not be.

He raised his glass. "To the third," he said.

Everybody stood. And Nina, who was twenty-four and had been drinking her mother's vodka since the soup, stood up too, and said in a clear voice that went right down the room, "And the fourth is Berlin."

There was a silence of the kind I described in Part I, the kind that a room makes when it is deciding what it has heard. Then Belov laughed, quietly, politely, and sat down, and the band began to play, and the moment was swallowed. But I saw Katya's face. It was turned to her sister with an expression I have only seen on the faces of icons, of a woman who loves somebody and has already seen what will happen to them.

Nina's wedding present to her sister was a novel by Sorokin, *Day of the Oprichnik*, in which Russia in the future has built a great wall against the West and is ruled by a tsar again, whose guards ride about in red cars with dogs' heads on the bonnets. It was meant as a joke. Katya read it on her honeymoon. "He writes it as a nightmare," she said to me afterwards. "Half of it is a plan." I have never been able to decide which half she meant, or which half she thought was the good one.

There is one more thing I saw at that wedding, and I set it down here because the reader will need it. At about midnight a tall man with a scar through one eyebrow came in late, in uniform, a colonel now, and kissed the bride's hand, and congratulated Belov, whom he seemed to know from some ministry or other, and asked the bride for one dance. They danced once. They did not speak at all. I watched Katya's face, as fifteen years before I had watched her mother's in the assembly hall, and her mother's face that night had shown nothing. Katya's showed something. It went over it once, like the shadow of a bird across a field, and was gone.

There is a line of Tyutchev's that every Russian schoolchild knows. Russia cannot be understood with the mind, nor measured with a common yardstick. In Russia one can only believe. That night, driving home along the coast road in the back of a taxi with my wife asleep on my shoulder, I said it over to myself, and it was Katya's face I saw.

## V

I must go quickly through two years, because the next thing that matters happened in a church.

In February 2007 the leader gave his speech at the conference in Munich, the one where he told the Americans to their faces that they had overstepped their borders in every way, and asked where the guarantees were that had been given after the Warsaw Pact dissolved. Konstantin watched it four times. In April 2008, at Bucharest, NATO declared that Georgia and Ukraine would one day become members. In August there was a war in Georgia, five days long. Katya was at the dacha on the lagoon when it began, on a week's leave, and she sat on the step of the shed where her mother had made the first vodka and listened to the radio with a face like a closed door.

"Bucharest was a promise," she said to her father, who was frying fish. "They promised Georgia and Ukraine something they had no intention of giving, and Georgia believed them."

"So it's the Americans' fault the Georgians shelled their own city?" said Nina, from the hammock. She had come out to the dacha with a book by Ulitskaya and had not been reading it.

"A child asks for a knife," said Katya. "A grown-up who hands it to him is responsible for what he does with it."

"Georgia isn't a child."

"Then why do they need a nanny with tanks?"

"Fish," said Konstantin, and put it on the table, and that was the end of it, because his fish was very good. I mention this only so that the reader sees how it was in that family. Every argument in that family ended in food. They knew the argument would still be there in the morning, and the fish would be cold.

## VI

And now Easter.

In the spring of 2009 Konstantin had been seeing a woman in Zelenogradsk for nearly two years. Her name was Alla, she had a hair salon by the promenade where the cats sleep on the benches, and everybody knew. I knew. The woman from the top flat knew. Taras Ivanovich knew, and once, in the courtyard, said something to Konstantin in Ukrainian that made him go white and walk away. Lyudmila Petrovna knew. She said nothing. She bought rye.

On Holy Saturday, the eighteenth of April, Konstantin went to Zelenogradsk in the afternoon and did not come back for supper. Lyudmila Petrovna baked her kulich, and painted her eggs with onion skins, and made her paskha in the wooden mould that had been her grandmother's, and at eleven o'clock she put on her white scarf and walked to the cathedral on Victory Square with Nina and with me. Katya was in Moscow.

Anyone who has not been to the midnight service at Easter in a Russian church cannot imagine what it is, and anyone who has will forgive me if I describe it badly. By half past eleven the cathedral was so full that people stood out on the steps and down into the square, each with a thin candle, unlit, the old women in front because the old women are always in front, the young men at the back because they are always at the back, children asleep on shoulders, soldiers from the garrison in a block of grey near the doors, a drunk being held up kindly by two strangers, girls in their mothers' scarves, and over all of it the smell of beeswax and wet stone and the sweet, cheap incense the priests buy by the kilo. Inside it was dark. At midnight the priests came out of the altar with their candles lit, and the light was passed from candle to candle through the whole crowd, out of the doors, down the steps, into the square, until the square was full of small flames shaking in the wind off the Pregolya. And then the priest cried, "Christ is risen!" and three thousand people answered, "He is risen indeed!" and he cried it again, and again, and the bells began.

Konstantin came in at about that moment. I saw him at the back by the doors, among the soldiers, in his good coat, with no candle. He had driven from Zelenogradsk. I do not know what had happened there that evening. Nobody knows. He has never said.

Lyudmila Petrovna saw him too. She did not hesitate. She turned round and walked the whole length of that cathedral, against the crowd, with her candle held up in front of her so that it would not go out, and people let her through, they did not know why but they let her through, and she reached him by the doors, and she kissed him three times, on the right cheek, the left, the right, which is the Easter kiss, and she said what you say, "Christ is risen."

You must answer, "He is risen indeed." Every Russian knows this. A child of three knows it. A drunk at a bus stop knows it. Konstantin Sergeyevich opened his mouth and could not say it. He stood there among the soldiers with his mouth open and the tears running into his moustache and he could not say three words, and she waited, holding her candle, and the bells went on ringing over both of them, and at last he lit his own candle from hers, because he did not know what else to do with his hands, and the woman from the top flat, who was standing near me, said, "Well. There."

The next afternoon he drove to Zelenogradsk and came back in an hour, and he went to the shed where Lyudmila kept the empty bottles and asked her how she wanted them washed.

He has washed them for eighteen years. He drives the van. He does the accounts in a school exercise book in very small figures. He carries the sacks. He sits beside her at the market on Saturdays and talks to the customers, because he is charming and she is not, and from that day, as far as anyone knows, he has not looked at another woman.

A year later, on the stairs, I asked him what had happened that night. It was the only time I asked. He thought for a while.

"Pasha," he said, "I lied to everyone for twenty years and it was nothing. It cost me nothing. And on that night, standing there, I found out that I had been lying to myself, and it was unbearable. It was like finding out you have been walking around for twenty years with your trousers down."

I thought of the old monk Zosima in *The Brothers Karamazov*, who says it to the old buffoon Karamazov, the father, the first time they meet. Above all, do not lie to yourself. The man who lies to himself and listens to his own lie comes to a point where he cannot tell the truth within him or around him, and so loses all respect for himself and for others, and having no respect, he ceases to love. I did not quote it to Konstantin. He had found it without me, at the back of a cathedral, with his mouth open.

## VII

In 2010 the vodka became legal.

I should explain. In our country a person cannot simply make vodka and sell it, however good it is. There is no licence for a small maker. You need a factory, and a great deal of money in the bank, and a great many papers, and the papers take years, and many people who start the papers are old by the time they finish. Lyudmila Petrovna had been selling her rye for eighteen years to people who came to the door, and to Taras, and to half the officers of the fleet, and to a deputy of the regional parliament who sent his driver, and everyone had looked the other way, because everyone was drinking it. In the spring of 2010 an inspector came. He was young and he did not drink. He wrote a great many things in a folder.

Lyudmila Petrovna rang Katya that evening. I know because I was in the kitchen, eating her pickled mushrooms, and she spoke on the telephone with her back to me for perhaps two minutes and said almost nothing. "Yes. Yes. A young one. No, I didn't. Yes. Thank you, daughter."

A month later she had a contract with a licensed plant on the Chernyakhovsk road, where her own rye would be made under her own eye by her own two men, in her grandmother's way, and bottled with a plain white label that said only ZORINA, and under it, smaller, RYE. Nobody heard of the inspector's folder again. Other people in the region, I learned, had waited four years for such a contract. Lyudmila Petrovna waited a month.

I write this down because a chronicler must write down what he does not like. I asked her once whether she had asked Katya what she had done. She was rolling dough. She did not look up.

"I didn't ask her," she said. "She didn't tell me. We are a family of people who don't tell each other things, Pavel Andreyevich, and we love each other terribly."

## VIII

In the summer of 2012 the Poles began to come.

There was a new arrangement that year, which meant that people who lived near the border on either side could cross it with a simple permit, without a visa, and the whole of Kaliningrad suddenly became "near the border." On Saturdays the Poles drove over in their small cars for our petrol, which was half the price of theirs, and our cigarettes, and our vodka. On Sundays we drove over for their cheese and their furniture and their H&M. For four years the border was something you crossed for groceries. My wife, God rest her, said it was the most normal thing that had happened in her lifetime.

Lyudmila Petrovna had a stall at the Central Market by then, on Saturdays, with Konstantin beside her talking, and Nina came on the Saturdays when the gallery was closed. Nina was thirty, a curator at the art gallery on Moscow Prospekt, still unmarried, which the woman from the top flat mentioned to me about once a month.

In the spring of 2013 a Polish woman began to come to the stall every four weeks and buy six bottles. She was tall, with short hair the colour of the rye before it is cut, and paint under her fingernails. Her name was Agnieszka Wróbel and she was a conservator of paintings at a museum in Gdańsk, and she bought the six bottles, she said in careful Russian, for her father, who said that Russians could do only two things well, and this was one of them.

"And the other?" asked Konstantin, delighted.

"Suffer beautifully," said Agnieszka, and Konstantin laughed so hard he had to sit down, and Lyudmila Petrovna, who never laughed at the market, laughed too.

Nina did not laugh. Nina looked at her. I was there. I had come for mushrooms. And I saw Nina look at her, and go on looking after Agnieszka had paid and gone, until her father asked whether she had been turned to salt.

Agnieszka came every four weeks after that. Then every two. Then she came on days when she bought nothing.

## IX

Easter fell on the same day for everybody in 2014, ours and the Catholics', the twentieth of April, which happens only now and then, and Lyudmila Petrovna said it was a sign and that Nina should bring her Polish friend from the museum to the Easter table. So she did.

It was five weeks after Crimea. I must describe that spring honestly, which means saying that almost everybody I knew was happy. They were happy the way people are happy when an old injury stops hurting. In the courtyard the children had painted on the wall, in letters a metre high, the two words that the whole country was saying that spring, Crimea is ours. Konstantin had a little flag of the Black Sea Fleet in the window of the van. Taras Ivanovich, whose sister lived in Poltava, had stopped coming to the market for a month, and then started again, and nobody mentioned it.

Katya came from Moscow. Belov did not. There were nine of us round the table in the kitchen with the windmills, under the icon of the Mother of God in the corner that Lyudmila Petrovna had begun keeping after Easter 2009: the parents, the two daughters, Agnieszka, Taras, the woman from the top flat, her son and I. We had the eggs, and the paskha, and the kulich with its white cap, and the rye.

It was Konstantin who began it, of course. He raised a glass to Crimea.

Agnieszka put her glass down. She did it politely. She did not say anything.

Katya noticed. Katya noticed everything. And she spoke to Agnieszka in a perfectly friendly voice, as one educated woman to another. "You think we stole it."

"I think," said Agnieszka slowly, "that in my country we know what it is when a big neighbour decides which parts of you are really his."

"In December," said Katya, "an American minister walked round the Maidan in Kyiv handing out buns. Then we all heard her on the telephone deciding who would run Ukraine, and saying what she thought of Europe in a word I won't use at this table. Then the elected president ran for his life. You can call that a revolution. I call it somebody else's hand in the pot."

"A hundred people were shot on the Maidan," said Nina. "Who chose them, Katya? Was that on the record?"

"It is being investigated."

"By whom?"

Taras Ivanovich put his hand flat on the table. He was sixty-seven then. "My sister in Poltava," he said, in Russian, "says the Russians are coming. My nephew in Kaliningrad says the Russians are already here and they are us. I have lived in this city forty-three years. I don't know who I am this spring. Lyuda, pour." And she poured.

And then Nina did a thing I had been waiting twenty years for her to do, without knowing I had been waiting.

She reached over and put her hand on Agnieszka's hand, on the tablecloth, between the kulich and the salt, and left it there, and said to her mother, "Mama. She isn't my friend from the museum."

I must remind the reader that it was 2014, and that the year before, the parliament had passed the law against what it called propaganda of non-traditional relations, and that the woman from the top flat was sitting at the table with her son. It was so quiet I could hear the tap dripping.

Konstantin spoke first. He looked at the two hands for a long time, and then he looked at his own, and then he said, in a voice I had never heard from him, "I have loved a great many women badly. If you can love one well, you are a better person than your father." And he drank, alone, and wiped his moustache.

Lyudmila Petrovna said, "I knew when you were nine. Eat."

The woman from the top flat ate. Her son ate. Taras crossed himself and ate.

Katya said nothing at all. She looked at her sister's hand on the Polish woman's hand, and she looked up at the icon in the corner, and then she cut herself a piece of kulich, and passed the plate to Agnieszka, and said, "Try the paskha. Mama's is the best in the city. Ask anyone."

Later, at midnight, she caught me on the stairs as I was going down. She stood on the step above me, as her mother did when she read me the letters, so that our heads were level.

"Pavel Andreyevich," she said, very quietly. "If anybody ever asks you, Agnieszka is Nina's colleague from the museum in Gdańsk."

"And if nobody asks?"

"Then you have never seen her." She kissed me on the forehead, which she had not done since she was eleven, and went back up.

## X

And now I must tell the thing I am least proud of, because I saw it by accident and I have never told anyone.

In June 2016 I went to Svetlogorsk for a week for my chest, which the sea air is supposed to help, and on the third morning, walking early on the upper promenade, I passed the small grey hotel at the end of it that the Moscow people use, the one with no sign. There was a black car outside with Moscow plates. And as I passed, a tall man came out of the hotel door, in civilian clothes, with a scar through one eyebrow. He was a general now. I had seen his photograph in the paper. He got into the black car and it drove away. I sat down on a bench with my newspaper. Ten minutes later Katya came out of the same door in a raincoat, with her hair wet, and walked straight along the promenade towards me, and stopped, and said, "Good morning, Pavel Andreyevich. The sea air is good for the chest," and walked on.

She did not ask me to keep quiet. She knew she had no need to.

I have taught *Anna Karenina* eleven times. I always told the children that it was a book about a woman who could not live a lie and so chose death, and a man, Levin, who could not live without meaning and so chose God, and that Tolstoy put them in one book so that we would see they were the same problem. That morning in Svetlogorsk, out of breath on a bench, I thought of Anna at the last station, deciding alone what she owed and to whom, because nobody in her whole world would help her decide it. And I thought: Katya will not throw herself under a train. She is too much a lawyer.

On the fourth of July, three weeks later, the Poles closed the border arrangement. They said it was because of the NATO summit in Warsaw. Our side said it was because of NATO, full stop. Either way, from that morning Agnieszka needed a visa to come to the market, and Nina needed one to go to Gdańsk, and the border went back to being a border.

Nina drove to the crossing at Mamonovo that day. I do not know why. There was nothing to see. She told me later she had sat in the car on our side and watched the Polish lorries going through, and that she had laughed, because the only thing that had been closed was the border for people who were buying petrol and vodka and cheese, and the lorries went on through as usual.

That evening, by chance, both sisters were in their mother's kitchen. Katya had come down from Svetlogorsk. Nina had come back from the border. Lyudmila Petrovna put the kettle on and the rye out, and the three of them sat under the cracked windmill without speaking, and I sat with them, because I had been passing and she had called me in.

After a long time Lyudmila Petrovna said, "Tolstoy says all happy families are alike." She turned her glass round on the table. "I've never met one. Have you, Pavel Andreyevich? You've read more than me."

I said I had not.

"Then he was guessing," she said, and poured.

## Epilogue

*What the story holds underneath, and where the tradition meets it.*

Part II is about what a family does with its failures: a husband's, a state's, its own. Below are the patterns underneath, named plainly, each with the pages on this wiki where the tradition speaks to it.

**Desire with no edges.** Konstantin's twenty years of women, counted by nobody. The tradition places [[Kama|kama]] third among the aims of life, after dharma and artha, so that desire has a boundary before it begins. Without one it becomes the first of the [[Shadripus|shadripus]], the six enemies of the mind, and it scatters a man until he cannot answer three words at Easter.

**The lie told to oneself.** "I lied to everyone for twenty years and it was nothing. And on that night I found out that I had been lying to myself, and it was unbearable." Zosima says it first: above all, do not lie to yourself. [[Satya]] starts with what a person will admit in private. The rest follows from there.

**Service after a fall.** For eighteen years Konstantin washes bottles, drives the van and keeps the accounts in very small figures. He asks nothing for it. The tradition calls this [[Seva|seva]], and the Gita calls the attitude behind it [[Karma Yoga|karma yoga]]: work done for someone else, with no eye on the reward. It is how he repairs what he broke.

**The favour.** One phone call, and a licence that takes others four years arrives in a month. Lyudmila does not ask how. "We are a family of people who don't tell each other things." This is [[Artha|artha]], wealth, bending dharma for the family's gain. The Gita warns against wealth gathered by crooked means (Bhagavad Gita 16.12). The story does not punish the favour. It asks the reader to notice it.

**A place at the table.** Nina puts her hand on Agnieszka's, between the kulich and the salt. Her father says she loves better than he did. Her mother says, "I knew when you were nine. Eat." In 2014, with a new law against it on the books, the family keeps its daughter. That is kuldharma, the second of the [[Four Dharmas]]. Katya's quiet word on the stairs is the same duty, done from inside the state.

**The officer and the vow.** Katya dances once with Vadim at her own wedding, and something crosses her face. Ten years later she walks out of a hotel in Svetlogorsk and greets her old teacher as if nothing were strange. Kama and kuldharma now pull in opposite directions inside one woman. Part III shows which one she keeps, and what it costs.
