# Wind of Change

*The Third Rome, Part I.*

**Before you read.** This is the first of three stories in *[[The Third Rome]]*. It is told by Pavel Andreyevich Shubin, a retired teacher of Russian literature in Kaliningrad. Kaliningrad is the small piece of Russia that sits on its own on the Baltic coast, between Poland and Lithuania. Until 1945 it was the German city of Königsberg. Pavel Andreyevich has lived one floor below the Zorin family since 1989, and he is writing in 2027, when the war has reached the city. Part I goes back to the winter of 1991, when the Soviet Union ended and the two Zorin daughters, Katya and Nina, were eleven and ten. Their father builds ships at a yard that soon stops paying. Their mother starts to make vodka. The elder girl begins to believe that the West broke a promise to her country. The younger one begins to love the West.

## I

I am not a writer, and I ask to be forgiven for it at once, because I am going to write a long thing all the same. For thirty-eight years I taught Russian literature at School No. 19 on Kutuzov Street, and a man who has explained Dostoevsky to fourteen-year-olds for thirty-eight years catches the bad habit of thinking he could do it himself. He cannot. I know this. But the Zorins have lived above me since the spring of 1989, in the old German house with the stone pears over the door, and someone ought to write them down before the drones finish what the British bombers began here in the August of 1944. My wife, God rest her, would have done it better. She could tell a story at table so that the soup went cold. I can only tell it the way I taught, which is to start with a crowd and wait for the faces.

So: the crowd.

By six o'clock on the twenty-seventh of December 1991 the assembly hall of School No. 19 was full, and still the parents kept coming in out of the snow, stamping on the mat that Fenya the caretaker had put down and that nobody wiped their feet on, the women in rabbit and the luckier women in fox, the men in naval caps if they had served and knitted caps if they had not, all of them carrying the cold in on their coats, so that the hall, which was heated by a German boiler older than anyone in it, smelt at once of wet wool and tangerines and the Polish perfume that had begun to arrive that autumn in the bags of the women who went over the border with vodka and came back with everything else. Two days earlier the red flag had come down over the Kremlin. We had watched it on the staff-room television, the director and I and the physics teacher, who cried, and Fenya, who said it was a pity about the flag because the cloth was good. Now the portrait of Lenin had gone from the wall behind the stage and left a paler square where it had hung for twenty years, and the director, who was a practical woman, had covered the square with a print of Shishkin's bears in the pine forest, so that the small children, who knew those bears from the wrappers of chocolates they could no longer get, looked at the wall and grew hungry. The radiators knocked. A baby cried in the third row and nobody minded. A captain from the fleet at Baltiysk sat in the front row with all his medals on, very straight, looking at nobody.

I had organised the concert, as I organised every concert, and I stood at the side of the stage with a programme I had typed myself on the school's one typewriter, the one whose letter ы stuck.

Lyudmila Petrovna Zorina sat in the fourth row by the aisle, in a grey coat she had let out twice, with a thermos on her knees. She was thirty-four then, broad in the shoulder, with a face that looked stern until she laughed, and in public she did not laugh often. The thermos held tea, she told the woman beside her. It did not hold tea. I know because later in the evening she poured some of it into the cap for me, and I drank it, and the inside of my head went warm and clear, as if someone had opened a window in it. It was rye, made by her grandmother's method from a village near Smolensk, and that was the first time I tasted what half of Kaliningrad would one day queue for. I did not know that then. Nobody knew anything then. Everyone had been told that the thing they knew was finished, and nobody had yet been told what came next.

The seat beside her, which she was keeping with her handbag, was empty.

The girls came on in the second half. Katya first. She was eleven and in the sixth class, a thin child with plaits so tight they pulled her eyebrows up, which made her look surprised. She almost never was. She recited Pushkin, the poem to his friend Chaadaev. I had chosen it for her in October, when it seemed safe, a young man's poem from 1818 about a star of happiness that would one day rise. By December nothing was safe. She stood at the front of the stage with her hands at her sides and said it to the captain, as if he alone had come.

> Comrade, believe: it will rise,\
> the star that takes the breath away,\
> and Russia will start up out of sleep,\
> and on the broken stones of tyranny\
> they will write our names.

When she reached Russia starting up out of sleep, the hall went quiet. I have taught that poem to perhaps a thousand children and I never heard a hall go quiet like that. The captain put his hand over his eyes. Katya did not look at him. She finished, bowed exactly as I had taught her, short and low, and walked off.

Then Nina.

I must confess that I had forbidden what Nina did next. She was ten and in the fifth class, rounder than her sister and louder, with her mother's laugh and her father's eyes, which were the kind that look at you as if you have already agreed. In November she had come to me with a cassette. She wanted to sing the song by the German band, the one about walking down to Gorky Park, which had played that whole year from every kiosk on Leninsky Prospekt. She had learned the English by ear, without knowing what most of it meant. I told her the concert was for Russian songs. She looked at me with her father's eyes and went to the director, and the director, who had covered Lenin with bears, said yes.

So Nina walked to the middle of the stage, put two fingers to her lips, and whistled the opening, that long thin whistle the whole country knew, and then she sang in an English that was half invented, and the hall did a thing I did not expect. It began to sway. Women who had stood in a queue for butter since five that morning swayed in their rabbit coats. Fenya swayed by the door. The captain did not sway. He stood up, put on his cap, and walked out up the aisle with his medals ringing a little, and as he reached the doors they opened from outside, and Konstantin Sergeyevich Zorin came in with snow on his shoulders and the smell of Climat on his collar.

I should explain to younger readers that Climat was a French perfume that every Soviet woman wanted and almost none had, because it appears in the film about the man who gets drunk in a bathhouse on New Year's Eve and wakes up in Leningrad. Lyudmila Petrovna did not own any Climat. Konstantin smelt of it all the same.

He was thirty-one, an engineer at the Yantar shipyard, tall, with a moustache he was proud of and a way of standing in a doorway as if it had been built for him. He did not sit down. He stood at the back while his younger daughter sang, and when she finished he clapped louder than anyone, and then, because he was Konstantin Sergeyevich and could never leave a room alone, he called out over all the heads, "Lyuda! I've bought a car!"

The whole hall turned round.

"An Audi," he told everyone. "Eighty-four. From Hamburg. It's outside. It has a cassette player." Then he looked up at Nina on the stage and said, more quietly, though everybody heard it, "You can play your Germans in it, little one."

Lyudmila Petrovna did not turn round. She unscrewed the thermos, poured a capful, and held it up over her shoulder without looking, and he came down the aisle and took it from her hand and drank it standing, and sat down beside her at last, and she moved her handbag. That was all. I watched her face. I have watched it for thirty-six years since, in good years and bad, and I can tell the reader that I saw nothing in it that night, nothing at all, and this nothing was the most frightening thing in the hall.

Katya saw it too. She was standing in the wings beside me, waiting to take her bow with the others, and she said, without turning her head, "Pavel Andreyevich, does Papa have another woman?"

I said I did not know.

"Then he has," she said. "Grown-ups say 'I don't know' in that voice when they know."

She was eleven.

## II

I must say a word about the house, because the house is half of this story.

It stands in Amalienau, the quarter the Germans built for professors and merchants a hundred years before us, on a street of lime trees that our planners renamed after a marshal. In 1947 the German families still living in it were put on trains to Germany, and Russian families from Smolensk and Belarusian families from Vitebsk and Ukrainian families from Poltava were put into it in their place, and the big rooms were cut in half with plywood. By 1989 it held four flats. I had the ground floor with my wife. The Zorins came to the floor above us from a hostel at the shipyard, with two little girls and a sewing machine and a crate of Lyudmila's grandmother's jars.

Their kitchen still had the German tiles, white, with small blue windmills, and Lyudmila Petrovna would not let anyone touch them. "They were here before us," she said. "They'll be here after." She said the same about the cathedral on the island where Kant is buried, and about the amber that the sea throws up on the beach at Yantarny, and, later, about the Germans themselves.

On the second of January 1992 prices were set free. After that the shops slowly filled up again, and nobody could afford what was in them. Konstantin's wages from the shipyard came in March for December, then in June for February, then in sugar. I am not inventing the sugar. For some months the yard paid its engineers partly in fifty-kilo sacks from a deal nobody understood, and the Zorins' hallway was stacked with them to the ceiling, and you went to the toilet sideways. And it was the sugar that started the vodka, though not in the way you think.

Any fool can make spirit from sugar, and in 1992 every fool did. Lyudmila Petrovna would not. "Sugar makes a drink that shouts," she said. "Rye makes a drink that talks." She traded the sugar, sack by sack, with a collective farm near Gusev that had rye and no sugar, and she took the rye out to the dacha they had on the lagoon near Polessk, and in the shed behind the dacha, with her grandmother's copper and her grandmother's words, she began.

I helped carry, the first summer. I remember the smell, which was the smell of bread just before it burns, and the birch logs that Konstantin split for charcoal on the Sundays he was at home, and the jars lined up on the windowsill, clear as water, with the evening light coming through them. I remember Lyudmila tasting from a spoon with her eyes shut, like a woman listening at a door. I do not remember how she made it, and if I did I would not write it down. It was hers.

Her first customer was Taras Ivanovich Hrytsenko. He was a mechanic at the fishing port, a Ukrainian from Poltava who had come to Kaliningrad in 1971 to repair trawlers and stayed because, he said, the sea here was the right grey. He came to the door of the dacha one evening in August with a bag of smoked bream, because he had heard. Lyudmila poured him a glass. He drank it, put the glass down, crossed himself, and said in Ukrainian, "That is my mother's horilka." Then he sat on the step and wept for a while, and nobody minded, and he went home with two bottles and left the bream.

"A Ukrainian," said Konstantin that night, amused. "Your first is a Ukrainian."

"Vodka has no passport," said Lyudmila Petrovna. It was the first time I heard her say it. It was not the last.

I will not count Konstantin's women in those years. I do not think he counted them either. He was laid off in 1994 and began to drive to Germany for used cars, Audis and Opels and once a Mercedes with a bullet hole in the boot that he said came from Chechnya and certainly came from a Hamburg dealer, and he sold them in Kaliningrad, and he was charming in two languages, and he came home late. I noticed only one thing about Lyudmila Petrovna in those years. Each time he came home late, she bought another sack of rye.

There is a line in Dostoevsky's book about the prison camp that I taught every year to the ninth class. Man is a creature that can get used to anything, and I think that is the best definition of him. I used to teach it as a sentence about prisoners. In the nineties I understood that he had meant it about us.

## III

The West came into that kitchen in two ways, and I must tell both, because one went into each girl.

The first came by the newspaper, in August 1993. Konstantin was reading at the table, which he did loudly, the way some men eat. Our president had been to Warsaw and, according to the papers, had told the Poles over dinner that their joining NATO was nothing Russia need fear.

"He was drunk," said Konstantin. "Of course he was drunk. In 1990 the American promised Gorbachev, not one inch to the east. Baker. James Baker. I read it. Not one inch. And now our Boris drinks with the Poles and gives them the inch and the mile."

Katya was thirteen. She was doing her algebra at the other end of the table with her elbow round the page, the way she always did, as if somebody might copy it. She did not look up. "Did he write it down?"

"Who?"

"The American. Did he write it down, the inch?"

Konstantin laughed. "Between gentlemen, one doesn't write it down."

"Then one of them wasn't a gentleman," said Katya, and turned the page.

Nina was eating bread with sugar on it, which was what we had in 1993. "Why shouldn't the Poles join what they like?"

"Because they're Poles," said her father, and roared at his own joke, and Lyudmila Petrovna said "Eat," which was what she said whenever politics came to the table, and for once everybody ate.

The second way came by the front door, in the summer of 1995.

It was an old man with a daughter of fifty and a camera. He stood on the pavement under the lime trees for a long time looking at the stone pears, and then he rang every bell. Lyudmila Petrovna was the one who came down. He spoke no Russian. Nina, who was thirteen and had two years of school German, stood behind her mother and translated with her face going red. His name was Wendt. He had been born in the room that was now the Zorins' kitchen, in 1931. He was thirteen in January 1945, when his mother walked him out across the frozen lagoon behind a handcart.

Lyudmila took him upstairs. I came up too, because I had heard the bells and because I am, I admit it, a curious man. He stood in the kitchen and touched the blue windmills one after another with two fingers, as if counting them, and then he sat down on Konstantin's stool and put his face in his hands. His daughter photographed the tiles. Lyudmila Petrovna took a bottle off the shelf and poured three glasses, one for him, one for his daughter, one for herself, and after a moment a fourth for me.

Katya came in from the courtyard and stood in the doorway and took it all in. She was fifteen. "They burned this city," she said in Russian, quite clearly, "and now they come to cry in our kitchen."

"He was thirteen," said her mother. "Pour him another."

Nina wrote to Herr Wendt after that. He wrote back from Lübeck, long letters in a large hand, and sent her books, Rilke and Heine, and later cassettes, and once, at Christmas, a box of marzipan shaped like small potatoes that she would not let anyone eat for a month. Her German grew. Her face changed when she spoke it, the way some people's faces change when they sing.

## IV

In the spring of 1996 Katya sat an examination at the Palace of Youth for an American programme that sent Russian schoolchildren to live with American families for a year. There were two hundred children in the hall and four places for the whole region. She wrote her essay on the question, What will Russia be in the twenty-first century? She told me afterwards what she had written. "I wrote that Russia will be what it decides to be, and that the problem of the twentieth century was that it let other people decide." She got a place.

She went in August to a town in Iowa called Pella, which, she wrote, was built by Dutch people and grew tulips and had a windmill, "like Mama's tiles, only big enough to live in." Lyudmila Petrovna read the letters to me on the stairs, standing on the step above so that our heads were level, and I have kept the sense of them though not the words.

The family was called Henderson. Doug and Karen, and a son, Ben, who was seventeen and played American football and wanted to join the army. Doug sold farm machinery and said a prayer before every meal, with his eyes closed and his big red hands flat on the table, and Katya wrote that it was more serious than anything she had ever seen in a Russian church, "and Mama, I have been to a Russian church perhaps four times." Karen drove her to a shop called Walmart in the first week, and she stood in the cereal aisle and could not move, because there were, she wrote, ninety kinds of breakfast and not one of them was porridge. Ben taught her to drive his pickup on the gravel roads between the cornfields. She taught Ben to drink her mother's vodka, a bottle of which she had carried across the Atlantic in a boot wrapped in tights.

At Christmas came the letter that Lyudmila read twice.

"They are kind, Mama," Katya wrote. "They are kind the way people are kind when nothing bad has happened to them for fifty years. Ben asked me last night whether we are still their enemies. I said we never had the time."

And in the spring, another, shorter. "In history class the teacher said that NATO is like a club that countries ask to join. I asked her why, in that case, the club keeps building its new doors next to our house. She said it was a good question. They always say that here when they don't want to answer."

She came home in June 1997, at seventeen, with American teeth, which is to say she smiled more, and a decision. "Law," she said to me on the stairs, the first evening. "Everything in America is a contract, Pavel Andreyevich. Even love. Even church. I want to learn to read contracts so that nobody can ever again sell us a thing with a handshake."

I noticed something else. In America she had gone every Sunday with the Hendersons to their church, which was Reformed, with no icons and a coffee machine. She came back Orthodox. She began to go to the old church on the edge of the park on Saturday nights and stand at the back with her head covered, though nobody in her family had prayed since her great-grandmother. I asked her why. "Because they have nothing to stand on," she said. "They are good people standing on nothing. I don't want to stand on nothing." I have seen the same thing happen to other young people who went to America. I have never heard it explained.

Nina, meanwhile, was fifteen and in love with everything Katya had been sent to see and had come back distrusting. She wore jeans her father brought from Hamburg. She read Herr Wendt's Rilke with a dictionary. She and her friend Olya rode the tram to the Polish market holding hands, the way Russian girls always have, and I would not mention it at all except that I was her teacher and I noticed that in her case it was a different thing, and I said nothing, and I was right to say nothing. That too belongs later.

## V

In August 1998 the rouble fell through the floor. Konstantin had lost his savings once already, in 1994, in Mavrodi's pyramid, the one with the advertisements about Lyonya Golubkov buying his wife a pair of boots. He lost what he had rebuilt in a week. Lyudmila Petrovna lost nothing, because she had never put a kopeck in a bank. Her money was in dollars, in a three-litre jar under the German floorboards beneath the stove, and when the rouble fell her customers began paying in dollars too, or in diesel, or in a pig, and she went on.

I would like to say that this was the year Konstantin changed. He changed later, at an Easter service, and it is the only miracle in this book.

The first scandal I must describe properly came in March 1999. It was Lyudmila's birthday, the twenty-third, and I had been invited upstairs, and so had Taras Ivanovich, who brought bream as always. Katya was nineteen and in her second year of law at the university. Nina was seventeen and reading Pelevin's new book about the young man in advertising, and laughing out loud at it in a way that annoyed her sister.

The television was on in the corner. It is always on in a Russian kitchen. It is like a second stove. At about ten o'clock the news said that our prime minister, Primakov, had been flying to Washington, and that over the Atlantic he had been told by telephone that the Americans would begin bombing Yugoslavia, and that he had ordered the pilot to turn round. They showed a drawing of the plane making a loop over the ocean.

Konstantin put down his fork. "He turned round," he said. "Did you hear? He turned round over the ocean. That is the first time in ten years a Russian has gone home with his face." He was moved. He stood up. He made a toast to Primakov that lasted four minutes.

Katya did not drink to it. She had a small notebook beside her plate, the kind she always carried, and she wrote in it, and later, clearing the plates, I saw the page. *12 March. Poland, Hungary, Czech Republic. 23 March. The loop.*

The bombing began the next night. But the scandal was at that table, on the twenty-third, and it was Nina's.

"The Serbs are driving the Albanians out of Kosovo," she said. "Herr Wendt says it is like what happened to his family. People with bags on the roads."

Konstantin turned round slowly. "Your German," he said. "Your German says."

"He was on those roads, Papa. In 1945. He was thirteen."

"And the Serbs are our brothers, and the Germans bombed Belgrade in 1941 too, and now they will bomb it again with the Americans, and you sit at my table and tell me what your German says." And he threw his glass. He threw it hard at the wall, and it hit the tiles above the stove, and one blue windmill cracked from corner to corner.

Nobody said anything. Taras Ivanovich looked at his bream.

Then Katya spoke, in the calm voice she had already learned at the university, the voice of a person reading out a clause. "There is a principle in law," she said. "If you make a promise, and the other side relies on your promise and changes its position because of it, you cannot go back on the promise afterwards, even if nothing was written. In English it is called estoppel. We gave them Germany. We took our army out of the whole of Europe. We relied on the promise. Now the promise has gone back on itself, one country at a time."

"Listen to her," said Konstantin, still breathing hard. "Listen to the lawyer."

"And if the Poles relied on a promise too?" said Nina. Her face was white. "Whose promise wins?"

Katya looked at her sister for a long moment. "The stronger one's," she said. "That is what I am learning."

Lyudmila Petrovna got up then. She did not say "Eat." She took a cloth and a dustpan and swept the pieces of the glass from under the stove, and she ran her thumb along the crack in the windmill as if checking whether it would cut, and she said to nobody, "Twenty years I have kept politics out of this kitchen." She never replaced the tile. It is cracked still. I looked at it last night.

## VI

The last scene of this part is the shortest, because it was the shortest day.

On the thirty-first of December 1999, at noon by Moscow time, which is eleven by ours, the whole house was in the Zorins' kitchen, because theirs was the warmest, and because Lyudmila Petrovna had been making salad Olivier since seven. Taras Ivanovich came. The woman from the top flat came with her son. Konstantin was carving a ham he had brought from Germany. And the president came on the television, without warning, and said that he was leaving.

Everybody now remembers him saying, "I am tired, I am leaving." He never said "I am tired." I have the recording, I have listened to it many times. He said that he was leaving, and that he had done what he could, and he asked us to forgive him for the dreams that had not come true. But the whole country heard "I am tired," because the whole country was.

And then, at midnight, another face, the new one. A young man by our standards, pale, in a dark suit, speaking without any paper, short sentences, no jokes. I will not write his name. Everybody knows it, and in my old age names have become expensive.

"He doesn't drink," said Lyudmila Petrovna, after watching for a while. "Thank God. Somebody in this country will be sober."

Konstantin laughed and kissed her on the top of the head, and she let him.

Nina, who was eighteen, raised her glass of Soviet champagne and said, "To the year 2000. In Berlin they're dancing at the Brandenburg Gate."

"Then let them dance," said her mother. "We'll drink."

But I was watching Katya. She was looking at the new face on the television the way a girl at a dance looks at a man she has already decided to marry.

I went downstairs at two in the morning, a little drunk, and I sat in my own kitchen for a while in my coat. I was thinking about the old Inquisitor in *The Brothers Karamazov*, the one who tells the returning Christ that people do not want freedom, that freedom is too heavy, that they want three things only, miracle, mystery and authority. I had seen the faces in that kitchen when the new man spoke. I had felt it myself. It was relief, the relief of a tired man when somebody else picks up the bag.

Above my head I could hear Lyudmila Petrovna washing up, and the water running, and then the scrape of a stool as she stood on it to wipe the tiles above the stove, the cracked one too, gently, the way you would wipe a child's face.

## Epilogue

*What the story holds underneath, and where the tradition meets it.*

Part I is the soil the rest of the story grows in: hunger, a promise with nothing written, a husband who strays and a wife who says nothing. Below are the patterns underneath, named plainly, each with the pages on this wiki where the tradition speaks to it.

**Silence that is not yet forgiveness.** Lyudmila smells another woman's perfume on her husband and pours him a drink. For years, each time he comes home late, she buys another sack of rye. The narrator calls her blank face the most frightening thing in the hall. The tradition counts forgiveness, [[Kshama]], among the ten marks of dharma. Swallowed anger is something else. It goes into the body, or into work. Lyudmila's goes into a business, which is a better place than most. The real forgiveness comes in Part II, and it costs her a walk the length of a cathedral.

**Hunger the body keeps.** Wages paid in sugar, bread with sugar on it for supper, dollars in a jar under the floor. Thirty years later Lyudmila packs for a daughter as if for the camps, "which is what our bodies remember." Scarcity leaves a [[Samskaras|samskara]], an impression that goes on shaping choices long after the danger has passed. The tradition starts with food and the body, the first of the [[Pancha Kosha|five sheaths]], because that layer remembers first. The [[Annamaya Index]] takes this further.

**A promise with nothing written.** At thirteen Katya asks whether the American's inch was written down. At nineteen she calls it estoppel. The sense of a promise broken becomes the floor of her politics, and of her family's. The tradition's word for speech that lines up with action is [[Satya]]. The story leaves open who broke faith with whom. It is clear about what the feeling does to a girl who decides never again to trust a handshake.

**Two daughters, two Wests.** An old German counting tiles in a kitchen gives Nina her West. A newspaper and a year in Iowa give Katya hers, a West she learns to distrust. Each daughter follows her own nature, and neither tries to become the other. The Gita calls this swadharma, the first of the [[Four Dharmas]]: better your own path walked imperfectly than another's walked well.

**Relief at a strong hand.** On the last night of 1999 a new, sober face comes on the television, and the narrator feels relief, "the relief of a tired man when somebody else picks up the bag." Dostoevsky's Inquisitor says people will hand their freedom to whoever brings miracle, mystery and authority. After ten years of chaos the mind sinks into [[Tamas|tamas]], heavy and tired, and wants someone else to carry the weight. The tradition's guard is [[Buddhi|buddhi]], the discerning mind, which keeps asking even when it is tired.

*Nothing here is a treatment. If one of these patterns is close to your own life, speak to a teacher, or to someone trained to help.*
