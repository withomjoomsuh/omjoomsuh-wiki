# A Fourth There Shall Not Be

*The Third Rome, Part III.*

**Before you read.** This is the last of three stories in *[[The Third Rome]]*. It begins on 24 February 2022 and ends on Easter night, 2 May 2027. The last two years are an imagined near future, in which the war has reached Kaliningrad itself. Drones come at night. On the other side are Ukrainian units, a German brigade from Lithuania and an American who commands a drone unit. North Korean soldiers stand with the Russian army in the city's defence. Nina has gone to Berlin with Agnieszka. Katya, deep inside the state, has to weigh the people she loves against the duties she has taken on, and one night she holds a piece of paper that could end a life. None of this future has happened. The epilogue reads Katya's choices through the four dharmas and the four aims of a human life.

## I

On Easter night this year the sirens began at a quarter past eleven, and nobody went home.

I want the reader to see it. Victory Square, where Lenin stood and where the white cathedral stands now, dark, because the city has been dark at night since March, the street lamps off, the shop windows taped, the windows of the flats round the square blacked out with blankets and cardboard and in one case, I noticed, with a large poster of a pop singer turned to face the room. And in the dark the crowd, thicker than I have ever seen it, filling the steps, filling the square, spilling into Prospekt Mira as far as the tram stop, thousands of people standing close in their coats with unlit candles in their hands, because the priests had said the service would go on whatever came over, and the people had decided to believe them. Old women with stools. Mothers with sleeping children wrapped in blankets the colour of the dark. Sailors from Baltiysk. Two policemen with their caps off. Taras Ivanovich, eighty years old, on his stick, who had not been inside our cathedral since 2014 and came anyway. Students who had never been to church and had come because, as one of them told me, if something hit the cathedral tonight he wanted to be inside it when it happened. And at the corner by the tram stop, behind the metal barriers, the cordon: a few of our soldiers and, a little apart from them, a dozen young men in a uniform slightly different from ours, with smooth, closed, very young faces, posted there to guard the square, who looked up at the gold domes and the moving candles and the black sky with an attention I have only ever seen on the faces of children at the circus.

The sirens went on. Somewhere out towards the port the guns began, that dry knocking that we have learned to tell from thunder. Nobody moved. A baby woke and cried and was rocked. And at midnight, exactly, the doors opened and the light came out.

But I must go back five years and come forward again, so that the reader knows who was standing in that crowd, and why.

## II

On the twenty-fourth of February 2022 the telephone rang upstairs at a little after three in the morning by our clocks, which run an hour behind Moscow. The ceiling of my bedroom is the floor of Nina's old room, and the old German boards carry everything. I heard Nina's voice go up, and stop, and then Lyudmila Petrovna's steps in the corridor.

It was Katya. I learned later what she had said, because Nina told me, on the pavement, with the engine running. She had said: "Go to Agnieszka today. Take the car. Go while nobody is counting yet."

Nina had been living half in Gdańsk and half here for six years by then, on visas, with a toothbrush in each city. Agnieszka had just taken a post at a museum in Berlin. "Why today?" Nina had asked. And Katya had said, "Because tomorrow it will be a decision. Today it is still a trip." A little before five the leader came on the television and spoke for half an hour, and after that nobody in the house asked Katya how she had known.

Lyudmila Petrovna packed for her daughter as if for the camps, which is what our mothers' mothers did and what our bodies remember. Two bottles of the rye wrapped in jumpers. A jar of salted mushrooms. A piece of salo in newspaper. The good knife. Konstantin brought the van round to drive her to the border at Mamonovo, because he said he did not trust her car, which was a lie. He could not bear to let her go alone.

And then Lyudmila did a thing I did not expect. She went to the stove, and stood on the stool, and with the blade of a butter knife she worked one of the blue windmill tiles off the wall above it. One of the whole ones. It came away with a little sigh of old plaster. She wiped it with her apron, wrapped it in a clean tea towel, and put it in Nina's hands.

"For Herr Wendt's daughter," she said. "In Lübeck. Tell her it was theirs."

Herr Wendt had died in 2011. Nina had been to the funeral. She stood in the kitchen holding the tile against her chest in its towel and could not speak, which in Nina was something I had never seen.

On the pavement she hugged me and said, "Pavel Andreyevich, you taught us that Russian literature is all about people who cannot leave."

"Three sisters," I said. "To Moscow, to Moscow. They never go."

"I'm going," she said, and got into the van. It was still dark. Konstantin drove her to the border. He came back at noon and washed bottles till evening without speaking, and Lyudmila let him.

There is now a gap in the wall above the stove, beside the cracked windmill, where you can see the grey plaster the Germans laid in 1906. Lyudmila Petrovna has never filled it. She says it is a window.

## III

In June of that year Katya came home by train.

She had no need to. Belov's office could have put her on a plane, and since February the planes had been flying round Lithuania and Poland, out over the open sea, an hour longer. She chose the train. She told me afterwards that she wanted to think, and that a train is the last place where a Russian can think for twenty hours without a telephone, because between Belarus and Kaliningrad, crossing Lithuania, the signal comes and goes and the doors are locked.

This needs explaining to foreigners. The train from Moscow to Kaliningrad crosses Lithuania, which is in the European Union and in NATO, and the Lithuanians let it pass on condition that nobody gets off. At each Lithuanian station the train stops, and the Lithuanian border guards walk through it, and the doors stay shut, and you look out of the window at a platform in a country you are not in. That June the Lithuanians had also stopped certain goods coming through to us by rail, and our television was full of it. Katya was going home for Trinity, which fell that year on the twelfth of June, our national day, and she had a great deal to think about, because that spring the general had asked her to leave her husband.

I know this because she told me, much later, on a night I will come to.

At Vilnius the train stood for twenty minutes. It was evening, warm, the light long and yellow across the platform. She went and stood at the end of the carriage by the door. It was locked, of course. She tried the handle anyway. She told me she did not know why she tried it. And then she began to laugh, there by the door, alone, so that a Lithuanian border guard coming through the carriage, a woman of fifty with grey hair in a knot, stopped and asked her something in Lithuanian, and then in Russian, whether she was all right.

"I was thinking of a novel," Katya said.

The guard looked at the locked door and at Katya's face, and said, "Tolstoy?"

"Yes."

"We read it here too," said the guard. "In school. In Russian." She tapped the door with one finger. "My grandmother was put on a train in 1949. From this station. To Irkutsk. The doors were locked then also." And she nodded, quite politely, and walked on down the carriage checking passports.

"In Tolstoy's time," Katya said to me, when she told me this, "a woman could at least choose her train. In ours the Lithuanians lock the doors. And they have their reasons." She was silent for a while. "I was angry with that guard for a year. Then I stopped being angry, and that was worse."

## IV

In December of that year the former German chancellor gave an interview to a German newspaper, and in it she said that the agreements signed at Minsk in 2014 had been an attempt to give Ukraine time, and that Ukraine had used the time to grow stronger.

Katya brought the interview to her mother's kitchen, printed out, in German, with lines underlined in green. Nina was on the laptop on the table, from Berlin, with Agnieszka's shoulder at the edge of the screen. It was the first time the sisters had argued since February, and they argued the way they always had, as if they were nine and ten and at the same table, which, in a way, they were.

"She has admitted it," said Katya. "Minsk was signed to buy time. We signed in good faith. They signed to rearm. In my profession there is a name for signing a contract you never meant to keep."

"She said in the same interview that it was an attempt to prevent this war," said Nina from the screen. "Read the whole thing, Katya."

"I have read it in German."

"So have I. I live in it."

Konstantin said, "Fish," though there was no fish, and they both laughed, and that was the end of it. But afterwards, on the stairs, Katya said to me, "She lives in it. Did you hear? She lives in German now." And she said it in a voice I could not read at all, and I have been reading that family for thirty-eight years.

## V

In the autumn of 2023 the son of the woman from the top flat started a channel on Telegram.

He was thirty-six, the same boy who had sat at the Easter table in 2014 and eaten his paskha in silence, and he had grown into one of those men who defend the motherland very busily from their kitchens. His channel had eleven thousand subscribers and a name with the word Bastion in it. In August he posted photographs that he had found, he said, on the open internet, of Nina in Berlin, in July, at the big parade they have there, holding hands with Agnieszka under a flag of many colours. Underneath he wrote that Nina's mother's vodka was sold in our city, and named it. ZORINA. RYE.

At the end of November the Supreme Court declared what it called the international LGBT movement to be extremist.

On the first of December he sent his photographs, with the court's decision attached, to the regional prosecutor's office. He told his mother so. His mother told me, on the stairs, white in the face, and asked me whether I thought he had done right, and I said I thought he should be ashamed, and she began to cry and said she knew.

And then nothing happened.

I mean exactly that. Nobody came to the plant on the Chernyakhovsk road. Nobody rang Lyudmila Petrovna. The post about Nina disappeared on the fourth of December, and the channel itself a week later. In January he moved to Moscow. His mother said he had been offered work there. She would not say what kind, and I think she did not know.

I was a schoolteacher for thirty-eight years. I know what a drawer is. A letter can go into one in an office in Moscow, and the drawer can be locked, and the key can sit in the pocket of somebody who will never be asked about it. Katya came home that New Year and said nothing about any of it, and neither did her mother, and neither did I. I have lived below them so long that I have caught their habit of not asking.

What it cost, I learned three and a half years later, on Easter night.

## VI

In the spring of 2027 the war came north.

I am not going to explain how. I do not know. Each side has a film that proves the other fired first at the Suwałki crossing, and both films are very convincing, and I am seventy-nine and have stopped believing films. What I know is what came over our roofs. The airport closed in March. The German brigade that had been standing in Lithuania since 2025 stopped standing. The Ukrainians, who by then had drones that could fly the length of the Baltic, began to send them at night. And our army, which had been thin here since 2022 because it was needed in the south, was joined at the end of March by a battalion of soldiers from North Korea, who came by sea to Baltiysk in a grey ship and were driven inland in lorries, and nobody in the city had ever seen one before.

A squad of them was put on the Chernyakhovsk road, because the plant where Lyudmila's rye is made stands beside a fuel depot. They lived in tents in the field behind it. The first week, Lyudmila Petrovna sent Konstantin out with a pot of borscht and twelve spoons. The second week she went herself.

One of them was a boy called Ri Chol-jin. He was twenty and looked fifteen. He had no Russian, and Konstantin had no Korean, and they became friends in an afternoon, the way Konstantin always made friends, with his hands and his face. Ri showed him a photograph of his mother, a small woman in front of a row of big clay jars in a yard. Konstantin brought him into the shed and showed him Lyudmila's grandmother's jars, the old Smolensk ones, glass, and the two of them stood in front of the jars nodding at each other for a long time. Their officer, a thin man with a little Russian, allowed the soup and nothing else. But one afternoon, when the officer had been called away to Chernyakhovsk, Konstantin poured the boy a very small glass of the rye. Ri drank it, shut his eyes, opened them, and said a long sentence in Korean that nobody understood, and then he put his hand flat on his chest, and then flat on the bottle, and laughed.

"He understands," said Konstantin at supper. "A boy from the other end of the earth, and he understands."

On the night of the fourteenth of April a drone came down on the store at the plant, where the rye waits before it is milled. Nobody was hurt. Ri's squad put the fire out with sand and the plant's two hoses before the fire brigade came. For three days the whole of that road smelt of burnt bread.

On the Friday, Taras Ivanovich came to the flat for his bottle, as he has come every Friday for thirty-five years. He came up the stairs one at a time on his stick, and stood in the doorway of the kitchen with his cap in his hands, and said, in Ukrainian, then in Russian, "Lyuda. My grandson Andriy flies the things that come at night. His mother told me. He is in a unit, over there. Maybe it was him. I don't know. Forgive me."

Lyudmila Petrovna took a bottle off the shelf and put it in his hands and closed his fingers round it, and when he reached for his money she pushed his hand away.

"Vodka has no passport," she said.

"And grandsons?" said Taras.

"Sit down," she said. "Eat."

## VII

Katya came home for Easter on the Saturday, on a plane of the fleet that flew a long way round over the sea and landed at the military field. She came up the stairs in a dark coat, carrying a cake box from Moscow, and she looked very tired, older than forty-seven, and she kissed her mother and her father and me, and then she went and stood in front of the gap in the tiles above the stove and put two fingers into it, and stood there for a while.

And so to that night, and the crowd, and the light.

When the doors opened at midnight and the priests came out with their candles and the flame went from hand to hand down the steps into the square, I was in the choir, in the bass, where I have stood since 1993, so I saw it from above. I saw the light reach the Zorins. Konstantin lit his candle from a stranger's and turned and lit Lyudmila's, and she lit Katya's. And then Katya did a thing I would not have thought of. She went to the barrier at the corner, where the cordon stood, and held her flame out across it to the nearest of the young Koreans, who had no candle. He looked at his sergeant. The sergeant looked at the sky. An old woman beside Katya pushed a spare candle into the boy's hand, and he lit it from Katya's. It was Ri. I learned that afterwards. And the light went along the barrier from him to the next boy, and the next, a dozen small flames on a dozen closed young faces, and the guns went on knocking out by the port, and the priest cried "Christ is risen!" and the square answered "He is risen indeed!" so loudly that I could not hear the guns.

Lyudmila Petrovna had her telephone in her other hand, held up, with the screen towards the crowd. On the screen, very small, was Nina, in Berlin, in a kitchen, with Agnieszka beside her holding up a kulich she had baked from Lyudmila's recipe, lopsided, with its white cap sliding off one side. Nina said, "Christ is risen, Mama." And her mother, standing in the dark under the sirens with her candle, said, "He is risen indeed, daughter."

Then Katya gave the Easter kiss. Her father, three times. Her mother, three times. She came up the steps and kissed me, three times, an old man in the choir, which is not done, and the priest pretended not to see.

When the crowd was going home I passed the barrier. The boy was still holding his candle, cupped in his hand against the wind, though it had burned almost down to his fingers. His sergeant had let him keep it.

At three in the morning we were round the table in the kitchen. The sirens had stopped. Kulich with its white cap, the paskha in its wooden mould, eggs dyed with onion skins, and the rye from the last crate that had come out of the store before the fire. Taras was there. The woman from the top flat was there, who had not been invited for three years and was invited that night, and sat at the end of the table and did not look up. Nina was on the laptop, propped against the kulich.

Konstantin made the first toast. He was sixty-six. He stood up slowly and held up his glass and looked round the table for a long time.

"To those who are not here," he said. And everybody drank, and nobody asked who he meant, because everybody had somebody.

It was then that I saw it. I had sat at that table, under that icon, a hundred times. In the corner, on the little shelf beside the Mother of God, where Lyudmila Petrovna keeps the lamp and the dried willow from Palm Sunday, there was a photograph in a cheap gold frame, of Nina and Agnieszka on a beach somewhere, in the wind, laughing, with their arms round each other.

I must have looked at it for too long. Lyudmila Petrovna saw me looking.

"Since 2014," she said quietly. "You never noticed?"

I said I never had.

"Nobody does," she said. "It's next to God. Nobody looks there." And she passed me the eggs.

## VIII

At about five, when the window was going grey, I went down the stairs, and Katya came after me, and we stood on the landing between the two floors, by the window that looks out on the lime trees.

She told me then about the drawer. I will set it down the way she said it.

"Gennady found out," she said. "He finds everything out. It is his gift. He came to my room in the flat in Moscow in January 2024, at eleven at night, in his dressing gown, with his ears, Pavel Andreyevich, you remember his ears. And he stood in the door and said, quite gently, 'I am not a monster, Katya. I am a Russian husband. I can bear anything except being laughed at. Your general laughs at me at dinners. Everybody knows. And your sister's letter has cost me a favour from people who do not forget favours, and I will be paying for it for years, and I will pay. I will pay gladly. But you will stop.' And he went back to bed."

She looked out at the lime trees.

"I rang Vadim from the car the next morning. Outside the office, with the engine running. I said, 'I have a sister.' That was all I said. Four words. And he understood at once. That was the worst of it. He understood at once, and he said, 'Yes,' and he put the phone down, and I have not spoken to him since, except once, at a funeral, about the weather."

I said nothing.

Then she took a piece of paper out of her coat pocket. It was folded small, in four. She did not unfold it. She held it in her fingers on the windowsill between us.

"There is a criminal case in Moscow," she said, "against foreign mercenaries. That is what we call them. They are tried in their absence. One of the files is an American. Former US Army. He commands a drone unit of volunteers, and they think it is one of the units working this coast." She said the name in English, with the American r she had brought back from Iowa at seventeen. "Henderson. Benjamin. There is a photograph in the file. He has got old, Pavel Andreyevich. He has his father's jaw now." She turned the paper over. "He writes to me every March, on my birthday. Two lines. Happy birthday Katie, hope your folks are OK. He wrote in 2022 and he wrote this March. He has never once said what he does, and I have never once asked." Her fingers tightened on the paper. "This is his number. The people who want it do not know that I have it. It is my duty to give it to them. With a number they can find a man."

I asked her, God forgive me, what she was going to do.

She did not answer that. She said, "Do you know when I first read the Bhagavad Gita?"

I said I did not know she had read it at all.

"In 2011. There was a case in Tomsk. The prosecutor there wanted a Russian translation of it banned as extremist. I was given the file to look at, in Moscow, to see whether it would embarrass us with India. It would have. It was thrown out. But I read the book, to know what I was writing about, and I have read it every year since. Arjuna stands in his chariot between the two armies and he sees his teachers on the other side, and his cousins, and his old friends, and he puts down his bow and says he will not fight. And Krishna tells him he must. Better your own duty done badly, Krishna says, than another man's done well." She smiled. "Arjuna had his teachers across the field. I have a boy who taught me to drive a pickup on the gravel between the cornfields."

She looked at me.

"What does Dostoevsky say, Pavel Andreyevich? You always had a line."

I thought. And I gave her the only one I had, the one from the dying boy Markel, the brother of the old monk Zosima, which Zosima repeats all his life. "He says that each of us is guilty before all, for all, and that if people knew it, the world would be paradise tomorrow."

"That's no help," said Katya.

I had nothing better to give her.

She put the paper back in her pocket and kissed me on the forehead and went up the stairs to her mother's flat, and I heard the door close.

I saw the paper. I did not see what she did with it.

In the morning she flew back to Moscow on the plane of the fleet, round over the sea. On the Tuesday night the drones did not come. On the Wednesday night they came again. I do not know what that means. I am seventy-nine, and I have stopped deciding what things mean.

## IX

And now let me say what I have to say, and stop, because my chest is bad and the reader is tired.

They will say in Moscow that the Zorins are patriots, and they will say in Berlin that they are victims, and in Kyiv they will say something else again, and in Iowa, if anybody in Iowa ever hears of them, which God forbid, they will say whatever Iowa says. I have known the Zorins for thirty-eight years and I will say only what I saw.

What I saw was this. A woman made rye vodka in a bad century, in a shed by a lagoon, and sold her first bottle to a Ukrainian, and gave her last one to the same Ukrainian for nothing. Her husband could not say three words at the back of a cathedral, and has washed her bottles every day since. One daughter carried a German tile back to Germany in a tea towel. The other locked a drawer in Moscow for her sister and paid for the key with the one thing she ever wanted for herself. And there is a photograph next to the Mother of God, where nobody looks.

If that is not Russia, then I have taught the wrong books for thirty-eight years.

People say that Dostoevsky wrote that if there is no God, everything is permitted. He never wrote that sentence. Ivan says something near it, in several places, and the whole world has been quoting a sentence that is not in the book for a hundred and fifty years. Our president never said he was tired. The American's inch was said and never written down. We all live on sentences like that, on both sides of every border.

The old monk Zosima has another line. What is hell? he asks, and answers: the suffering of being unable to love. By that measure there was no hell in that kitchen at three in the morning, with the guns stopped and the eggs on the table and the laptop leaning against the kulich, though there was a war on the roof.

In 1880, a few months before he died, Dostoevsky gave a speech in Moscow about Pushkin, and the hall wept, and people fainted, and strangers embraced in the aisles. He said that to become fully Russian means to become a brother to all people. I remembered it at that table, thinking of the boy at the barrier with his candle burning down to his fingers, and looking at Agnieszka's face on the screen.

Two Romes have fallen, the old monk of Pskov wrote to the grand prince, and the third stands, and a fourth there shall not be. Gennady Arkadyevich toasted it at a wedding as a boast. I think the monk meant it as a warning: if this one falls, there is nothing after it. For most of my life I assumed he meant a city. I think now he meant a table like that one, with the people round it who should be round it, and the ones who should be round it and cannot be, propped up against the bread.

## Epilogue

*What the story holds underneath, and where the tradition meets it.*

This is a story about what holds a family together when the state, the war and the century all pull it apart. Sanskrit has one word for that. **[[Dharma]]** comes from the root *dhṛ*, to hold. It means whatever keeps a thing being what it is.

The tradition gives dharma four circles, one inside the other: duty to your own nature, to your family, to your work, and to your wider people. See [[Four Dharmas]]. Katya lives inside all four. The story is the record of where they pressed on each other.

**Swadharma, her own nature.** At eleven Katya hears a lie in an adult's voice. At thirteen she asks whether the inch was written down. She is a reader of contracts and a holder of lines, and she never tries to be anyone else. She never tries to be Nina. The Gita says it twice, in chapters 3 and 18: better your own dharma done imperfectly than another's done well (Bhagavad Gita 3.35 and 18.47, *śreyān sva-dharmo viguṇaḥ para-dharmāt sv-anuṣṭhitāt*). Even her faith comes from her own nature. She goes to Iowa, meets good people standing on nothing, and comes home needing ground under her feet.

**Kuldharma, her family.** She keeps her marriage to Belov, a vow made in a cathedral, though she loves another man. She keeps her sister safe through the drawer. She comes home under fire for Easter. The price of the drawer is Vadim. She pays it in four words, "I have a sister." That is kuldharma at full cost.

**Varna dharma, her work.** In the Gita, varna is your type by temperament and action, *guna* and *karma*. Birth does not decide it (Bhagavad Gita 4.13 and 18.41). Katya is a guardian by temperament, and she serves the state that way. The story does not pretend she keeps this circle clean. The phone call that gets her mother a licence in a month is family bending the state for its own gain. So is the drawer. A reader in the tradition should feel uneasy at both, and the narrator does. The paper on the windowsill is this circle at its hardest. It is Arjuna's moment. Her work tells her to hand over the number. The man on the other side of the field once taught her to drive.

**Jaati dharma, her people.** This is the widest circle, and here the collision happens. In 2023 the state named her sister's love extremist. If jaati dharma meant obeying the law of that season, Katya broke it. In the lineage reading, jaati dharma is duty to your people, your language, your faith and your land, and to the larger family of the earth. Dostoevsky said the Russian version of this in 1880: to become fully Russian is to become a brother to all people. Katya kept that circle. She kept it by protecting the very person the law had marked. [[Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam]] is the Indian name for the same idea.

**How the circles hold.** The tradition teaches that the outer circles rest on the inner ones. Katya's inner circles held. So when the outer ones pulled, her nature and her family set the limits of what the state could ask of her. That is why she is the moral centre of the story, lapses and all.

The tradition also names four aims of a human life, the [[Purusharthas|purusharthas]]: dharma, artha, kama and moksha. Dharma comes first so that the other three have a boundary before they begin. The family carries all four.

**Artha, wealth.** Belov is [[Artha|artha]] with no limit except the fear of being laughed at. He is the oligarch Russia, correct and cold, a man who pays for favours and keeps the receipts. Lyudmila is artha earned by skill. She builds a business out of sugar sacks and rye, and she sells to anyone who comes to the door. "Vodka has no passport" is artha that stays inside dharma.

**Kama, desire.** The story holds three kinds. Konstantin's twenty years of women is kama with no boundary, and it scatters him. Katya's love for Vadim is kama she gives up so that kuldharma can stand. Nina and Agnieszka are kama inside dharma. Their love builds a household, keeps a place at a mother's table and returns a tile to Germany. They are the story's good marriage, the Levin and Kitty of this *Anna Karenina*. See [[Kama]].

**Moksha, release.** Nobody in the story leaves the world. [[Moksha]] shows up small. Konstantin cannot answer "He is risen indeed" and is freed, in that moment, from lying to himself. He spends eighteen years in one-pointed service to his wife's work, which the tradition calls [[Seva]]. Lyudmila's forgiveness, at the back of the cathedral in 2009, is [[Kshama|kṣamā]], one of the ten marks of dharma in the Manusmriti (6.92). Easter night is the nearest the family comes to freedom: guns on the roof, and nobody in the kitchen unable to love.

**What is left open.** We never learn what Katya did with the paper. The Mahabharata says the truth of dharma is hidden in a cave (Vana Parva, the questions of the Yaksha: *dharmasya tattvaṃ nihitaṃ guhāyām*). A tradition that wanted a rulebook would not have said that. Krishna's answer to Arjuna is about how to act: without hatred, and without clinging to what follows. See [[Karma Yoga]].

**The nationalism of the table.** This is the moral, and the narrator says it plainly at the end. The Zorins' love of Russia lives in rye made the old way, in the Easter kiss, in Pushkin by heart, in a mother's paskha, in a Ukrainian customer served for thirty-five years, and in a photograph beside the Mother of God. That is jaati dharma as it is actually lived, by ordinary people, across a lifetime. It is wider than any state's version of it, and it outlasts each one.

**For the reader.** The [[Four Dharmas]] page asks four short questions. Ask them today. What do I owe my own nature? What do I owe my family? What does my work ask of me? What small act today holds together the larger world I belong to? When two answers pull against each other, as Katya's did, the inner circles come first. They are what make the outer ones possible.
