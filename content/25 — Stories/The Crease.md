# The Crease

*A story in seven folds, as I remember it.*

**Before you read.** This is the first of three stories in *[[The Second Sun]]*. Minato is eleven and lives in Tokyo in 2026. On the evening his father tells the family they are moving to Hiroshima, he falls asleep and dreams a whole other life. In the dream he is a boy born in 1923. He grows up through Japan's war years, lives through the morning Hiroshima was bombed, and in 1958, in a shed behind his house, makes the first noodles you cook by pouring on hot water. A paper crane, a grey heron, an old stone well and a second sun in the sky cross over from the dream into the waking world.

## Saturday evening

I was eleven, and on the last Saturday of October I pitched four innings for my junior baseball team in Tokyo and gave away two runs. One of them came off a ball I was sure was a strike, and I am still sure. My right arm ached all the way home on the train.

We lived on the ninth floor of a building in Kōenji, a busy old neighbourhood in the west of Tokyo, with the orange trains of the Chūō line running past the window so close you could count the people standing inside them. When I came in, the sun was going down, and it was shining straight into the bronze glass of the new tower across the tracks, and for a few minutes there were two suns in the sky. The real one, white and low. And its reflection in the glass, smaller and pale brown, the colour of weak tea.

Mei, my little sister, five years old, pressed her nose to the window and asked me which one was the real one. I pointed at one. Then I was not completely sure, so I did not say anything else.

At six the doorbell rang. It was a man from NHK, the national broadcaster, collecting the fee that every home with a television has to pay. NHK had stopped sending collectors from door to door some years back, and had only just announced that it would start again. He was thin, in a grey suit gone shiny at the elbows. My mother told him we had already paid. He checked his tablet, apologised, and said, almost to himself, "It's for the air, really. The air goes past whether you watch or not." Then he bowed to my mother, and then, oddly, to me as well, and left.

Dinner was grilled fish and rice, and then persimmons, Mei's favourite. My father waited for the persimmons. Then he put down his fork and told the persimmons that his company was moving him to Hiroshima in April, and that the whole family would go.

My mother already knew. She kept cutting fruit nobody had asked for. Sōta, my big brother, fourteen, said Hiroshima has the Carp, meaning the city's famous baseball team, meaning he was fine with it. Riku, seven, asked if Hiroshima had a river. Mei asked if we could take the cat. We did not have a cat.

I said nothing. I thought about my team, and the practice ground behind the shrine near the park, and the old stone well in the corner of that shrine, with a wooden lid and a rope tied round it, which the coach had told us a hundred times never to open.

After dinner Mei sat on the floor and folded a paper crane out of a supermarket leaflet. The neck came out bent. I told her it was wrong. "No," she said. "It's asking something." She put it on my pillow.

I went to bed early. On the shelf above my bunk stood the cup I had designed on a school trip to the Cupnoodles Museum in Yokohama, with a baseball drawn on one side and a grey heron on the other, the tall river bird that stands on one leg in the stream near our park. Above me Sōta was already snoring with his earphones in. Across the room, under his blanket, Riku was crunching dry Chicken Ramen straight from the packet. Some habits a family does not discuss.

I lay in the dark and the questions came in one by one, like mice. Whether there would be a team in Hiroshima. Whether I would be any good there. Whether I was even allowed to not want to go.

Somewhere in the middle of them, I fell asleep.

## The river ground, Osaka, 1934

It was 1934, and I was living in Osaka, in a wooden house that smelled of my mother's pickled plums and of the machine oil my father brought home on his sleeves from the cotton mill. I was eleven there too. I had been born in 1923, a few weeks after a great earthquake knocked Tokyo down, and my mother liked to say I came out angry because the whole country was still shaking.

Every evening, beside the river at the edge of the city, twelve of us boys played baseball on a patch of bare earth until the sun went down. The ball was cotton thread wound round a pebble. Home plate was a flattened biscuit tin with a stone on it. When I stood at the plate, nothing happened in my head at all. The ball came, the bat swung, and the whole world shrank to the two of us, like a fist closing. I never asked a single question on that ground. I have not been that quiet inside since.

A short train ride away stood a great stadium with an iron roof the grown-ups called the big iron umbrella. It was called Kōshien. Every August the school teams of the whole country played there, and we river-ground boys sat on the bank outside and listened to the roar come over the wall like thunder. I had decided that one day I would pitch at Kōshien. I told no one. You do not tell people the things you are sure of. You only tell them the things you need help believing.

That November a team of famous American players toured Japan, and the newspaper said a seventeen-year-old schoolboy, Eiji Sawamura, had struck out four of them in a row and then lost the game by one run. I cut the article out with my mother's sewing scissors and pinned it above my futon, and I read it every night, the way other boys said prayers.

On the second Tuesday of every month a man came to collect the radio fee. He was thin, in a grey suit gone shiny at the elbows. "It's for the air," he always said. "The air goes past whether you listen or not." He bowed to my mother, and then, for no reason anyone knew, to me.

In December my father came home and sat down at the low table without taking off his coat. He told the teapot that his company was sending him to Hiroshima in February. I waited for the rest of the sentence. There was no rest of the sentence.

The radio man came once more, in January. My mother told him we were moving. "The fee will follow you," he said. "Hiroshima has the same air." He looked at the ball in my hand. "Wherever they send you, the air goes past."

I did not understand. I kept the words anyway, in the drawer where I kept the things I would understand later.

## The bridge, Hiroshima, 1937

Hiroshima sat on seven rivers like a hand laid flat on a table, my father said on the train, and it was true. The rivers ran through the city like the gaps between fingers, and twice a day the sea came up them and went down again and left the mud shining.

There was no ground to play on. The army had the big field below the castle for marching, and the rest was rice fields and houses and Ujina, the port at the bottom of the city, where ships went out full of young soldiers with their names sewn inside their caps. I threw my ball against the wall of a soy-sauce godown until the owner came out in his apron and asked me, very politely, whether I meant to knock it down. So I stopped. And when the ball stopped, the questions started.

The first one came at school. Every morning eight hundred of us boys bowed together to a small stone shrine by the gate, where a photograph of the Emperor was kept in a shiny black box. One morning, halfway down, I wondered whether the photograph could see us through the stone. And if it could not, who we were bowing to. And whether I was the only boy who had ever wondered this, in which case something was wrong with me. I stood up late. On my report card someone wrote in red ink, lacks spirit. I kept that card for years. I was strangely proud of it.

After that the questions came like mice into a house in winter. You hear one, then you see one in daylight, and you understand there have been dozens all along. Whether the boy on the river ground in Osaka was really me, or someone I had borrowed for a while and now had to give back. Whether my father wanted this city, or had only said yes to the teapot. Whether Sawamura was frightened, facing the Americans. Whether a person was even allowed to wonder that.

The heron came in my third autumn there.

It stood in the river under the Aioi Bridge, the T-shaped bridge in the middle of the city, grey and still, on one leg, exactly like the bird on a cup I had never seen. One afternoon, when nobody else was on the bridge and the streetcar had just clanged away towards the station, it turned its long neck and looked at me and said, in a dry voice like someone speaking through a paper screen, "This one, or that one."

I was not surprised. That surprised me a great deal.

The heron never answered anything. It only asked. Both. Neither. Does it matter. Sometimes it asked whether I should be here at all, which was the worst one. It never followed me home, but I could feel it standing in the river behind me like a word left out of a sentence.

That December the army took Nanking, the capital of China, and all of Hiroshima came out at night with paper lanterns on poles and marched through the streets singing. Thousands of small orange lights swayed above the rivers. I marched with my class. Around me every face, lit from below, knew exactly what it felt. I held my lantern and felt the heron standing in the black water under the bridge, and I decided as I walked that this was my illness. Everyone else had certainty. I had a heron.

## The paper shop and the well

The paper shop was in a lane off the main shopping street, between a clock mender and a sock shop. It belonged to a widow called Mrs. Kinoshita, who had a face like a walnut and fingers that never stopped moving. She sold handmade paper and ink and envelopes, and she folded while she talked, without ever looking at her hands.

The fifth time I went in, I asked her how she knew where the fold goes. "I don't," she said, and gave me a square of paper the colour of weak tea. "You make a crease and ask it. If it's wrong, you open it out." She showed me. A faint line stayed down the middle, like a path across a field after one person has walked it. "That stays. Leave it alone. It's the paper asking."

My first crane came out with a bent neck. I said it was wrong. She put it on the shelf above the cash box anyway, and it stayed there for years, head tilted to one side, looking at me every time I came in.

In the back room there was a stone well with a wooden lid. Mrs. Kinoshita soaked mulberry bark in its water to make her paper, and the room smelled of wet wood and cooked rice. Once, when she thought I was not looking, I saw her drop a finished crane into it. I asked her why.

"Some folds don't get finished here," she said. "The well takes them somewhere they can be finished." She put the lid back on. "Don't open it unless you have to."

I went every week after that. I learned the square base, the bird base, the frog base, the shapes every paper animal starts from. I learned that you begin every animal without knowing what it will become, that the not knowing lives in the creases, and that a sheet with no creases in it can never become anything at all. I learned to hold the paper lightly and my first idea even more lightly. On the days I folded, the heron's questions stopped sounding like accusations. They sounded like questions.

Once I asked her whether she ever worried she was doing it wrong. "Every time," she said. Then she told me to pass the glue.

The war grew. Paper was rationed. I folded whatever I could find, mostly ration envelopes and the backs of notices about fire buckets. In 1944 the students were sent to the factories, and I stood eleven hours a day at a lathe, a machine that spins metal while a blade cuts it, making rifles at Toyo Kogyo on the eastern edge of the city, a company that had started out making cork. A lathe asks no questions. For whole hours my head went as quiet as it once had at the plate, and I caught myself missing the heron. I am not proud of how much I liked that quiet.

In the spring of 1945 the city pulled down whole rows of houses so that fires would have nowhere to spread, and Mrs. Kinoshita's lane was one of the rows. I helped her push the last of her paper in two handcarts to her sister's village in the hills. Before we left she gave me the crane with the bent neck. I put it in my shirt pocket. At the door she looked back once at the well, standing alone in the half-wrecked room, and left the lid on.

## The light, 1945

The sixth of August was a Monday. Clear, and already hot.

At a quarter past eight I was at my lathe by the east windows of the factory. I remember no sound. Other people speak of the sound, and I believe them, but in my own memory there is only light, a white so complete that for an instant I could see the bones of my hand through the skin. Then the windows came in like a wave.

I walked into the city that afternoon. My mother had been at home.

There is a part of this I never folded, and I am going to leave it the way I left it. Flat. White. Some things a person cannot say, or write, and I never tried. A black rain fell on my shirt and left marks that never washed out. I searched for her for six days. It seemed never-ending, and every moment of it felt as long as the whole Manhattan Project, from start to finish. My father found me on the fourth, and after that we searched together, and on the seventh we stopped. Neither of us said so out loud. Both of us kept that sadness stacked in our drawers for years.

On the seventh day I walked through what had been the lane off the main street. The clock mender was gone, and the sock shop, and the paper shop. Only one thing still stood, the round stone rim of Mrs. Kinoshita's well, black with soot, its wooden lid burned away. I looked down into it. Far below, the water was still there, dark and still, holding a small round piece of the sky.

In the water there were two suns.

I looked up. There were two in the sky as well. The usual one, high and white. And a second, smaller, a little to the west, the colour of the first square of paper Mrs. Kinoshita ever gave me. It cast no shadow, and it did not move when the first one moved. It was simply there, and it stayed.

I asked a cart driver if he could see it. He looked at the sky, then at the stains on my shirt, and said nothing, kindly. At the Red Cross hospital a doctor wrote one word on a card, shock, and handed it to me like medicine. After that I stopped telling people.

On the fifteenth of August, at noon, the whole factory stood around a radio while the Emperor spoke to the country for the first time. The radio crackled so badly that almost no one could make out the words. The air going past, I thought. People cried before they knew why. I stood with my hand over my shirt pocket, where the bent-necked crane lay flat, and looked out through the empty window frame at the second sun.

The heron was back in my head. It asked only one question now, and it asked it for years.

Why you.

## The shed, Osaka, 1958

Thirteen years went by.

I went back to Osaka with my father in 1946, because he had a cousin there who mended bicycles. That first winter, under the railway tracks at Umeda, I stood in a black-market queue for a bowl of noodles made from American flour. It went round two corners. In front of me were a soldier home from China without a coat, a woman with a baby tied to her back, two old men sharing one cigarette, and a boy of about nine who kept counting his coins. I waited an hour and ten minutes. The bowl lasted four minutes. I stood afterwards with the empty bowl still warm in my hands, doing the arithmetic.

I worked at a printing press, took the paper scraps home wrapped in the cloth my lunch box came in, and folded them at night. I married Fumiko, who worked in the office, who laughed at my paper frogs, and who said I was the only man she knew who apologised to a piece of paper before folding it. On our fourth evening together I told her about the second sun, sure she would get up and leave. She shaded her eyes and looked for a long time, and asked if it was warm. It was not. "Then leave it," she said. "It isn't using anything up." I think I married her for that sentence. The rest came later.

In 1957 I built a shed behind our rented house and set out to make noodles a person could keep in a cupboard and, on the coldest night, without a pot or a stall or an hour in a queue, simply pour hot water over. I thought about the boy counting coins.

For a whole year nothing worked. Sun-dried noodles cracked like old plaster. Steamed noodles, salted noodles and smoked noodles all tasted like wet cardboard, and Fumiko told me so every time, chewing slowly with her eyes shut. I wrote each failure on the shed wall in pencil. Forty-one lines. The heron took to standing outside the shed door at night, asking. I left the door open. I found I did not mind.

One evening in April I sat on the kitchen step and watched Fumiko fry tempura, slices of vegetable dipped in batter and dropped into hot oil. The batter puffed up and turned gold, and through the bubbles I saw the water inside rushing out as steam, and wherever it left, it left a hole. A sponge, made by heat.

I ran to the shed in my socks.

I steamed the noodles and soaked them in chicken soup. Then I did what my hands had known for twenty years. I laid the noodles in a square and folded them the way you fold the first shape of a crane, corner to corner, edge to edge, into a nest that held itself together. I dropped the nest into Fumiko's oil. It roared and turned gold. When it had cooled and I broke it open, it was full of holes, a little brick that weighed almost nothing. I put it in a bowl, poured boiling water over it, covered it with a plate, and counted two minutes out loud. The water went back into the holes it had come out of. The noodles opened in the bowl like a folded thing opening.

Fumiko tasted it with her eyes shut for a long time. Then she opened them and said nothing at all, and I understood every word.

The first packets went out in August. People laughed at the price. Then they bought them. Then they bought more.

On the morning the first box left the shed, the heron stepped up onto the workbench among the pencil lines and the flour. It did not ask anything. It folded its long neck down, and then its legs, and then its wings, and kept folding, smaller and flatter and neater, until it was a single square of grey paper lying on the bench. It was still warm. I put it in my shirt pocket, next to the crane with the bent neck.

That night I took the train to Hiroshima. The city had been built again. Where the lane had been there was a car park, and in its corner, behind a low fence, the stone rim of the well, with a new wooden lid. I lifted the lid. Down in the dark water, small and steady, there were two suns, though it was night.

I took the grey paper out of my pocket, and the crane with the bent neck, and dropped them both in. I heard them land, very softly, as if on a pillow.

## Sunday morning

I woke up with my hand pressed flat on the chest of my pyjamas, over a pocket my pyjamas did not have.

Tokyo. The ninth floor. Above me the bunk creaked as Sōta rolled over with his earphones still in. Through the wall came the sound of the television, which meant Riku was up. There were crumbs of dry noodles on Riku's empty bed. On the shelf stood the museum cup with its grey heron. And on my pillow, right next to my face, lay Mei's pink crane with the bent neck, exactly where she had put it.

My right arm ached from the four innings.

I sat for a long time holding the crane. I could still smell hot oil. I could still feel the cold stone rim of the well under my hand.

Then I got up and went to the window. The sun was up over the city, white and low, and in the bronze glass of the tower across the tracks there was a second one, smaller and pale brown. I looked at it for a while. I did not need to ask which one was real. I let them both stay.

Back in the bedroom I opened Mei's crane, slowly, all the way, until it was a flat pink square again with SALE printed across it in red. The creases stayed. A whole web of faint lines, every fold she had made and the one she had made wrong. I did not know what it should become. I did not know if I would like Hiroshima, or find a team, or be any good there.

I made the first crease anyway, and asked.

By the time I heard my father in the kitchen, the square had become a crane again, with a straight neck.

At breakfast I asked him one thing, whether Hiroshima had baseball teams for boys my age.

He put down his tea and smiled for the first time since the persimmons. "Lots," he said.

Before anyone saw, I went back and put the crane on Mei's pillow, while she was still asleep. I am still not sure which sun is the real one. I have stopped minding.
