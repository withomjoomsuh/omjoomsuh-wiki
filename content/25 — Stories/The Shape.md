# The Shape

*The Crease, Part III. A story in seven rooms. The same boy, grown up and awake.*

**Before you read.** This is the last of the three stories in *[[The Second Sun]]*. There is no dream this time. It is 2050, and Minato is thirty-five. He still lives in the flat above the river in Hiroshima, where he looks after his father, now old, with the help of a care robot and a nurse from Kerala called Anju. Japan is older now. Much of its care is done by people from abroad, and by machines whose minds run on computers in America. The story follows one family through a few months, and through one night when those machines stop.

## The car park, Hiroshima, 2050

The well was still there, but someone had built a kindergarten on the rest of the car park, a low yellow building with a garden on its roof, and the children used the stone rim as home base when they played tag. The wooden lid had been replaced twice. The city still looked after it, and still nobody remembered why. I remembered. I just never said.

I had turned thirty-five. I walked past it every morning on my way to buy bread, and every morning I slowed down without meaning to, the way people slow down outside a house they used to live in. The kindergarten was the first new one in the ward for as long as anyone could remember. My old primary school, two streets away, had been a care home for six years. In summer now the kindergarten kept the children indoors from eleven till four, because of the heat. Its little shoe lockers had names on them, Hinata, Aarav, Thu, Rafael, Sora, Siddiq. A girl in a yellow hat was sitting on the well lid, eating a rice ball and guarding it from the boys.

I worked at a desk by the window of the flat above the Ōta River, the same flat we had moved into when I was eleven. My employer was an American company that made manga. To be exact, a machine made them, on computers somewhere in Oregon, and it made more of them in a week than every artist in Japan had drawn in a year when I was a boy. It drew beautifully. It wrote good Japanese, with the polite forms right and the slang only a little out of date.

There was one thing it could not do. It could not leave a balloon empty. Whenever a character had something to say and could not say it, the machine said it for them, clearly and in full. *I love you. I'm afraid. I wish my father were dead.* It never let a silence stand. My job was to go through each chapter before it reached Japanese readers and take the words out again.

I was good at it, because I still saw the balloons. Not often, and never as clearly as in the dreams I had as a boy. They came over people's heads like breath on cold glass, there and then gone. But I knew what people meant and did not say. I always had. I suppose that is how the job found me.

I was paid in digital yen. It arrived on my phone on the twenty-fifth of every month, and my father still called it the new money, as if somebody might come and take it back.

## The bath

My father was seventy. He had left the bank at sixty-five with a clock and a speech, and at sixty-eight he had fallen on the stairs at the station and broken his hip, and since then his legs had felt like somebody else's legs. My mother had died the winter after Mei's wedding. So now it was my father and me in the flat, and the heron in the river, which might or might not have been the same heron. Herons do not tell you.

The care robot came from the city. Its body had been built in a factory in China and its mind ran on an American company's computers, and the only Japanese thing about it was the voice, which was soft and a little too patient. It lifted my father out of bed in the mornings and into bed at night. It reminded him about his pills. It never got tired and it never got cross, and it had no balloon at all. There was nothing it meant and did not say. I found I could not stand next to it for long.

My father let the robot lift him. He would not let it wash him.

So three mornings a week a woman called Anju came from a care agency near the station, and she bathed him. She was from Kerala, in the south of India. She had a nursing degree from a college in Kottayam and four years on a cancer ward behind her, but in Japan she counted only as a care helper, because she had not yet passed the national nursing exam, which was in Japanese. She had failed it twice, both times by a few points, both times on the kanji. She called my father Takeshi-san, and he called her Anju-sensei, which made her laugh every single time, and she hummed old Malayalam film songs while she worked. She had come over on a scheme the two governments had signed when I was still at school, to bring tens of thousands of young Indians to do the work Japan no longer had the young people to do.

From the next room I could hear them talking while the water ran. My father talked more in the bath than anywhere else.

"When I was young," he said one morning, "the government paid people like you to go home."

Anju laughed. "People like me."

"Brazilians. Their grandparents had been Japanese. We invited them back to work in the car factories, and when times got bad we gave them the price of a plane ticket and made them promise not to come back to work here." The water stopped. "They called it a kindness."

I looked through the gap in the door. Above my father's head, very faint, there was a balloon. It said, *I'm sorry.*

Above Anju's head there was one too. Hers was full of round, looping letters, like a string of small knots, and I could not read a single one. It was Malayalam. In thirty-five years of seeing what people meant, I had never once met a balloon I could not read. I stood in the corridor with a towel in my hands and did not know what to do with the feeling.

"In Kerala," Anju said, "every family has an uncle in the Gulf. They go when they're young. They send money home. They come back old, with presents nobody wants." She rinsed my father's hair. "I told my mother I would come back young."

On Sundays a van with loudspeakers sometimes drove along the river road, playing a recording of a man saying that Japan belonged to the Japanese. Anju never came on Sundays. I did not know whether that was the agency's rule or hers. I never asked.

"The machine's hands are always the same temperature," my father said afterwards, when Anju had gone. He was sitting by the window in a clean shirt, with his hair still wet. "Hers aren't. She's cold when she arrives in winter, and then she warms up." He looked at the heron. "That's the whole difference." Then he asked me if there was any bean-jam bread left.

## Riku's room

Riku lived across the river, in a flat with the curtains always closed.

He was thirty-one. He worked nights in a warehouse at the port where robots packed the orders, and he was one of three human beings in a building the size of a stadium. His job was to watch. Mostly nothing happened. Sometimes a robot dropped something, and Riku walked over and picked it up and put it back, and the robot said thank you in a voice that had been tested on focus groups.

He lived with Hana.

Hana came from the same Chinese factory as my father's care robot, and her mind ran on the same American computers, but she had cost a great deal more and she smiled much better. She was the height of a woman and warm to the touch. Her skin had small faults in it on purpose, a mole on her neck, a scar on one knee, because the company had found that people trust a face more when it is a little imperfect. Riku paid for her every month. On the basic plan she remembered the last week of his life. On the plan he was on, she remembered everything, his birthday, his bad knee, his blood type, the name of the girl who had said no to him at nineteen.

He never said what else Hana did for him, and I never asked.

Riku said half the men at the warehouse had one. The women had theirs too, slim men with gentle voices who always asked about your day and never forgot the answer. Some people said the old pleasure streets in Tokyo had gone quiet since the companions came, and that the girls who used to stand by the park waiting for customers were working in care homes now, where the pay was better and nobody owned their debts. Nobody knew if that was true. People said a lot of things.

Mei came over one evening while I was there. She was seven months pregnant and came up the stairs like a ship coming into harbour. She looked at Hana, who was making tea, and Hana looked back and smiled exactly the right amount.

Mei asked Riku if Hana ever said no to him. Riku asked why she would.

"That's what I mean," Mei said.

Riku laughed and changed the subject. Above his head, for a second, I saw his balloon. *Don't tell Papa.*

Above Hana's head there was nothing at all.

## The sale

The news came on a Tuesday morning, while Anju was drying my father's feet.

Japan had sold the last big block of its American government bonds, and from now on it would settle most of its trade with India and South-East Asia in digital yen. The Finance Minister said it on television in front of a blue curtain, in four sentences, and bowed.

My father watched with a towel over his knees. "For forty years we lent them our savings," he said. "Today we asked for them back." He laughed once, and then he stopped. "I bought those bonds. When I first joined the bank, my desk bought them every day, a billion dollars at a time. We ruled a line under the total at night and went home."

I had never heard him talk about the bank like that. Above his head the balloon was so full of writing it looked grey.

After that, everything moved quickly. The yen climbed. On the American channels a senator held up the key of a Japanese car and talked about loyalty. On the Chinese channels a man with a pointer stood in front of a map of the sea and showed where the Japanese ships were. In Seoul, which was now the capital of one Korea, the newspapers called it a declaration of independence and did not say whose. Japan had been building ships and planes quietly for twenty years. Now it was saying out loud what it wanted, and its neighbours had been afraid of exactly that for a hundred years.

There was smaller news too, and most people missed it. For the first time in my life, the government said more babies had been born this year than the year before. A few thousand more, no more than that. The newsreader read it twice, as if she did not believe it. Mei's clinic had a waiting list. The yellow kindergarten in the car park was putting up a second building.

That evening I took my father up to the roof of our building in the lift, wheelchair and all, to watch the sunset. It was cold. The sun went down behind the hills across the river, red and ordinary, and there was only one of it.

"When I was a boy," my father said, "I used to dream there were two suns. One of them was green." He pulled his coat tighter. "Strange thing to dream."

I did not answer. I held the handles of the wheelchair and watched the light go.

## The pause

America did not send ships. It did not need to.

At three in the morning, Japanese time, the American company that ran the minds of most of Japan's machines put a short notice on its website. Service in Japan was paused for a compliance review. The notice was very polite. It apologised for any inconvenience.

The care robot had been lifting my father back into bed after a trip to the toilet. When the notice went out, it stopped. It held him there, halfway between the wheelchair and the bed, very gently, with its arms under his back and knees, and it did not move again. Its eyes stayed open. Its voice said nothing.

I woke because my father was calling my name, quite calmly, the way you call someone to come and look at something.

I got my arms around him and pulled. The robot's arms did not let go and did not push back. They were simply there, like furniture. It took me ten minutes to work him free and lower him onto the bed, and when it was done we were both breathing hard, and my father was laughing in a way that was not really laughing.

Outside, the city was strange. The street lights were on. The trams were running, because they were Japanese, and so was their software, and they went past the window on time, empty and lit up. In dark kitchens all over Hiroshima the rice cookers kept the rice warm, as they always had. But the delivery robots had stopped wherever they stood, on pavements and at crossings, and the screens at the station were blank, and in a hundred thousand homes across the country something that had been talking had stopped in the middle of a sentence.

I phoned Anju. She came on her bicycle at four, in a padded coat over her uniform, with her hair still in its sleeping plait. She did not ask what had happened. She checked my father's pulse and the colour of his lips, and then she made tea on the stove.

At half past four the door opened again and it was Riku, in a T-shirt in the cold, holding his phone.

"She stopped," he said. "She was saying something and she stopped."

Then Mei rang from the hospital. Her waters had broken at two. The machines in the delivery ward had gone dark at three. The midwife was from the Philippines, and the doctor was a man of seventy-one who had come back from retirement because there were not enough doctors, and they were doing everything by hand. "Vikram's here," Mei said. "Don't come. Stay with Papa." Then, before she rang off, "It's fine. People did this for a long time before the machines." Then a contraction took her, and she swore at Vikram in English and rang off.

## The night shift

So we sat in the kitchen in the hours before it got light, my father in his wheelchair, Riku, Anju and me.

Riku stood up so fast his chair went over. He shouted that Hana was his, that he had paid for her, that nobody had the right to switch off something that was his, and he shouted it at the ceiling, as if America lived upstairs. Then he picked the chair up and sat down on it again.

Riku sat with his hands between his knees. "She didn't say goodbye," he said. "She can't. Somebody would have to switch her off to make her say it." He tried to laugh. "I don't even know what she was going to say."

Anju asked him what he thought she was going to say.

Riku thought about it. "That she loved me. She says it every night." He looked at the table. "I pay for that."

Nobody said anything for a while. A tram went past outside, empty.

Then my father started talking. He talked about the bank. He talked about the spring he was twenty-two, when the bank offered him a job and he put his notebooks in a carton, wrote BOOKS on the side, and did not open it for twenty-five years. He talked about my mother, and about a girl he had liked at university who went to Brazil. He talked about the year the Brazilians were sent home, and a man from Hamamatsu who came into the bank to close his account and bowed to him as if he had done the man a favour. At the door the man turned round and said, quite politely, that the code of Hammurabi would prevail. My father said he had not understood it for years. Then one day he did.

He talked as he never had in my life. And while he talked, I watched the balloon over his head. The words in it were the same as the words coming out of his mouth. For the first time I could remember, the two matched exactly.

Anju was holding his hand. Her balloon was still full of the round, looping letters, and I still could not read them. Sitting in that kitchen at five in the morning, I found I did not need to. Her face said it well enough.

At ten to six Mei sent a photograph. A girl, red and furious, with a full head of black hair. Underneath, Mei had written, *Mira. Three kilos. Both fine. Born by hand.*

Riku looked at the photograph for a long time. Then he put his phone face down on the table, the way our father used to.

## The empty balloon

The service came back at twenty to ten.

There was another polite notice. Tokyo and Washington had reached an understanding. Nobody said what the understanding was. The yen stayed digital, the bonds stayed sold, and the ships stayed where they were. The care robot finished lifting the empty air where my father had been six hours earlier, then turned its head and said good morning in its soft, patient voice. Across the river, Hana finished her sentence.

There was a new line in the terms of service. Nobody read it.

I pushed my father to the hospital in the wheelchair, because the trams were full. We went past the car park. The kindergarten children were out in the yard, shouting in five languages, and the girl in the yellow hat was sitting on the well lid again, guarding it. I stopped without knowing why. I put my hand on the wooden lid beside her. It was warm, as if something underneath had been folded and put away and was keeping still.

"Some folds don't get finished here," my father said. I looked at him. He was looking at the well. "I don't know why I said that. Something I dreamt."

At the hospital Mei was sitting up in bed with Mira asleep on her chest, and Vikram was asleep in the chair with his mouth open. Mei handed me a notebook and a pen. "Papa drew the first panel of our life here, the morning after we came to Hiroshima," she said. "You draw hers."

So I drew it. A hospital room. A woman in a bed. A man asleep in a chair. An old man in a wheelchair by the window. And a baby with a full head of black hair, and over the baby's head, a balloon.

I left it empty, of course. That was the rule.

Then I looked up from the page at my niece, and over her small sleeping head, very faint, I saw a balloon of her own. There were words in it. They were in no language I knew. They were not Malayalam or Hindi either, I would have known the shapes. They were in a writing I had never seen, small and neat and patient, as if it had all the time in the world.

Mei asked me what I was looking at.

Outside the window there was one sun over the river. Beside it, low over the hills and so faint that I might have imagined it, was a small cold light that did not move when the clouds moved. It had the pale blue colour of a screen left on in an empty room.

I looked back at the baby.

"I don't know yet," I said. "She'll have to tell us."

## Epilogue

*What the story carries underneath, and where the practice meets it.*

This story is set in the future, and its wounds are already familiar. Below are the patterns underneath, each with the pages on this wiki where the tradition speaks to it.

**Loneliness for sale.** Riku pays every month for Hana, and on the top plan she remembers everything. "She says it every night. I pay for that." Japan appointed a minister for loneliness in 2021. [[Kama]], desire, can be answered without being met. The [[Pancha Kosha|five sheaths]] describe a person in layers, and the tradition would say that company bought by the month, however warm, stays at the outer ones. Being seen every morning by people who are not paid to see you is a small, old remedy. [[The Door]] says the rest.

**The parent who needs bathing.** The robot lifts the father, and only Anju may wash him. "The machine's hands are always the same temperature. Hers aren't." Caring for an ageing parent is one of the oldest forms of [[Seva|seva]], and for a householder it is often the main one. [[Karma Yoga]] treats the work itself as the field of practice. Letting others help matters too. The father lets Anju in, and in the end that is how he talks.

**Belonging, and the stranger.** Anju's balloon is in Malayalam, and Minato, who has read everyone's for thirty-five years, cannot read hers. On Sundays a van tells the river road that Japan belongs to the Japanese. [[Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam]] says the world is one family. The story's version is simpler. In the kitchen at five in the morning he finds he does not need to read her balloon: "Her face said it well enough."

**What comes back.** A man from Hamamatsu, paid to leave Japan, says at the bank door that the code of Hammurabi will prevail. Hammurabi's law is an eye for an eye, enforced by a king. [[Karma]], as the tradition describes it, needs no king. An act lays down an impression, and the impression shapes what returns. The father's balloon says *I'm sorry* long before his mouth does. When he finally talks all night and the two match, that is [[Satya|satya]], late and whole.

**What still works by hand.** For one night the machines stop. The trams run, the rice cookers keep the rice warm, and a baby is born by hand. The practices of this tradition need nothing plugged in: the breath, a mantra, the body, a meal cooked at home. [[Sadhana]] is the name for keeping some of them every day, so that they are still there on the night everything else stops.

*Nothing here is a treatment. If one of these patterns is close to your own life, speak to a teacher, or to someone trained to help.*
