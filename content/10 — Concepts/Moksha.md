# Moksha

## What it is

Moksha is release. In the final sense it is freedom from the round of birth and death. In a lived sense it is freedom from the bonds that ignorance and craving tie around a life. It is the fourth of the four aims of a human life, the [[Purusharthas|purusharthas]]. The first three, [[Dharma]], [[Artha]] and [[Kama]], are pursued in the world. Moksha is the aim that loosens the world's hold.

## Sanskrit and etymology

*Mokṣa* (मोक्ष) comes from the root *muc*, to free, to let go, to release. The related word *mukti* means the same. A *jivanmukta* is a person liberated while still alive. Patanjali uses a different word, *kaivalya*, the aloneness of the seer, standing in its own nature.

## Where it appears in the canon

Patanjali ends the *Yoga Sutras* with kaivalya: when the gunas have served their purpose and return to their source, the power of seeing rests in its own nature.[^1]

The *Mundaka Upanishad* describes the moment of release. When that which is both high and low is seen, the knot of the heart is cut, all doubts fall away, and a person's karma is spent.[^2]

The *Bhagavad Gita* closes its teaching with Krishna's promise: give up all dharmas, take refuge in me alone, and I will release you from every sin, so do not grieve.[^3] Earlier, the Gita names King Janaka as one who reached perfection through action, in the middle of a householder's life.[^4]

The *Tattva Bodha* describes the jivanmukta, the one freed while living, who goes on acting in the world without being bound by the action.[^5]

## What is contested

**Whether it was always one of four.** The older dharma literature speaks of three aims, the *trivarga*: dharma, artha and kama. Moksha was added later, making four. Scholars of the Dharmashastra trace the four-aim scheme to a later layer than the three.[^6]

**Whether it needs renunciation.** One strand of the tradition treats release as the business of the monk who has left home. Another, which includes the Gita's teaching of action and this lineage's work with householders, holds that it is open to people raising families and running businesses. Janaka is the tradition's own example.

## Why it matters

For a householder, moksha rarely arrives as a single event. It shows up small first: freedom from a habit, a resentment, a lie told to oneself for years. Each small release is a piece of the large one. The practice of [[Karma Yoga]], acting without clinging to results, is the Gita's way of building those releases into an ordinary day.
