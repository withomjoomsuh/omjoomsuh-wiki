# Purusharthas

## What it is

The purusharthas are the four aims of a human life: [[Dharma|dharma]], [[Artha|artha]], [[Kama|kama]] and [[Moksha|moksha]]. Duty, wealth, desire and release. The tradition treats all four as legitimate. A life is not asked to give up money or love. It is asked to hold them in the right order.

The order is the teaching. Dharma comes first so that wealth and desire have a boundary before the pursuit begins. Moksha comes last because it loosens the hold of the other three. Read this way, the four describe a whole human life, from earning a living to letting go of it.

## Sanskrit and etymology

*Puruṣārtha* (पुरुषार्थ) joins two words. *Puruṣa* means a person, a human being. *Artha* means an aim or object. A purushartha is "what a person seeks." The same word *artha* is also the name of the second aim, which can confuse a first reader: artha is one of the four purusharthas, and also part of the word for all of them.

The older texts speak of three aims together, the *trivarga*: dharma, artha and kama. With moksha added the set becomes the *caturvarga*, the four.

## The four

**[[Dharma]].** What holds a thing in its place. For a person, the conduct proper to them, their duties to themselves, their family, their work and their people. The [[Four Dharmas]] page sets out those circles.

**[[Artha]].** Wealth and livelihood, the means to provide for those who depend on you. Earned honestly, it is a duty in its own right.

**[[Kama]].** Desire, pleasure, love and beauty. The *Gita* has Krishna say that he is present in desire that does not go against dharma.[^1]

**[[Moksha]].** Release from the bonds that ignorance and craving tie around a life, and in the end from the round of birth and death.

## Where it appears in the canon

The *Manusmriti* records the argument openly. Some say the good is dharma and artha, some kama and artha, some dharma alone, some artha alone. Its own settled view is that the good is the three together.[^2]

Kautilya's *Arthashastra* tells the king to enjoy pleasure without going against dharma and artha, and not to live without happiness. It allows that the three may be pursued equally, since each is bound to the others, and then gives its own view that artha is the chief, because dharma and kama rest on it.[^3]

The *Kama Sutra* opens by placing its subject among the three aims and teaches that a life should give each its proper time.[^4]

The *Mahabharata*, in the Shanti Parva, stages a debate among the Pandava brothers and Vidura on which of the three aims is greatest. Each speaks for a different one, and Yudhishthira closes by pointing beyond all three.[^5]

The *Bhagavad Gita* gives the line that holds the scheme together: desire is not condemned, desire that runs against dharma is.[^1]

## What is contested

**Three or four.** The four-aim scheme is later than the three. Scholars of the Dharmashastra trace moksha's addition to a later layer of the literature, and the addition creates a tension the texts never fully resolve.[^6] The first three are pursued in the world. Moksha can be read as leaving them behind.

**Which comes first.** Kautilya puts artha first for a practical reason, that a starving kingdom keeps no dharma. The dharma texts put dharma first for a moral one. The *Manusmriti* settles for the three together. The *Kama Sutra* puts them in time, each in its season. All of these positions sit inside the tradition.

**Aims and stages of life.** Modern teaching often maps one aim to each stage of life: dharma for the student, artha and kama for the householder, moksha for the renunciate. The classical texts do not lay it out so neatly, and a householder is expected to keep all four in view at once.

## Why it matters

For a householder, the purusharthas are the most practical frame the tradition offers. They do not ask anyone to despise their salary or their marriage. They ask four questions, in order. Is this right? Does it provide? Does it give joy? Does it free me, or bind me tighter? When the answers pull against each other, the earlier aim sets the limit for the later one.

Swami Sivananda placed dharma at the highest altar among the four, and this wiki's essay [[The Fourth Room]] follows that reading into the question of money.
