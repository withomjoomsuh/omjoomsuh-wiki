# Kleshas

## What it is

The kleshas are the five afflictions that Patanjali names as the roots of human suffering: not seeing clearly, the sense of a separate "I", clinging to what is pleasant, recoiling from what is painful, and the deep fear of death. When a person reacts the same way for the hundredth time, the yoga tradition looks for the klesha the reaction grows from.

## Sanskrit and etymology

*Kleśa* (क्लेश) comes from the root *kliś*, to torment, to trouble, to stain. The five, in Patanjali's order:

- *Avidyā* (अविद्या), not-seeing. Taking the passing for the lasting, the impure for the pure, pain for pleasure, and what is not the self for the self.
- *Asmitā* (अस्मिता), I-am-ness. Mistaking the instrument of seeing for the one who sees. This is the working of [[Ahamkara]].
- *Rāga* (राग), attachment. Clinging that follows the memory of pleasure.
- *Dveṣa* (द्वेष), aversion. Recoil that follows the memory of pain.
- *Abhiniveśa* (अभिनिवेश), clinging to life. The fear of death, which the sutra says is rooted even in the learned.

## Where it appears in the canon

**Yoga Sutras 2.3** names the five. **2.4** makes avidya the field in which the other four grow, whether they lie dormant, thinned, interrupted or fully active. **2.5 to 2.9** define each in turn, and 2.9 describes abhinivesha as a clinging that flows on by its own momentum and is found even in the wise.[^1]

**2.10 and 2.11** give the method in two lines. In their subtle form the kleshas are dissolved by turning back towards the source. In their active form, as the waves they raise in the mind, they are reduced by meditation. **2.12** then ties them to [[Karma]]: the store of action has the kleshas as its root.[^1]

**In the [[Bihar School of Yoga]]**, [[Swami Satyananda Saraswati|Swami Satyananda]]'s commentary *Four Chapters on Freedom* treats the kleshas as the working map of practice, the material that sadhana is actually working on, layer by layer.[^2]

## What is contested

**How far the kleshas map onto modern psychology.** The fit is tempting and partial. Abhinivesha resembles what researchers have described in people who survived mass death. Robert Jay Lifton, who interviewed survivors of Hiroshima in the early 1960s, described a "death guilt" and a "psychic numbing" that lasted for decades.[^3] The two frameworks were built for different purposes. This wiki uses the resemblance as a bridge for understanding and makes no claim that one proves the other.

**Whether the kleshas can be removed or only weakened.** Patanjali's word *tanu*, thinned, in 2.4 suggests a gradual process. Commentators differ on whether complete removal is possible short of liberation.

## Why it matters

**It names reactions that feel like character.** A woman who snaps over a spilled cup is rarely angry about the cup. The klesha map asks what lies underneath: an aversion laid down long ago, or a fear that has little to do with the kitchen.

**It explains why understanding is only a first step.** Sutra 2.11 says the active forms are reduced by meditation, which in practice means a daily discipline kept over years.

**It joins the inner work to the outer life.** Through 2.12 the afflictions are linked to [[Karma]] and to the [[Samskaras]] that action lays down. The same five roots run under both.
