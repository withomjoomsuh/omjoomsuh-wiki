# Seva

## What it is

Seva is service given without asking for anything in return. In the Indian traditions it is a practice in its own right: caring for others as a way of loosening the hold of the self. For a householder, the first field of seva is usually the home.

## Sanskrit and etymology

*Sevā* (सेवा) comes from the root *sev*, to attend upon, to serve, to honour. The same root gives *sevaka*, one who serves.

## Where it appears in the canon

**Bhagavad Gita 3.10 to 3.13** describes a world sustained by offering. Those who eat what remains after an offering are freed, and those who cook only for themselves, the Gita says, eat sin.[^1] Seva is that offering made with the hands.

**In the lineage**, [[Swami Sivananda Saraswati|Swami Sivananda]] summed up his teaching in six words: serve, love, give, purify, meditate, realise.[^2] Service comes first. Late in his life [[Swami Satyananda Saraswati|Swami Satyananda]] left the work at Munger and settled at Rikhia, where service to the surrounding villages became the centre of his work.[^3]

**Beyond the lineage**, seva is central in several Indian traditions, most visibly in the Sikh langar, where anyone may eat and anyone may serve.

## What is contested

**Seva and unpaid labour.** The same language has been used to make unpaid care work, most of it done by women, easier to demand. A word that names a freely given practice can be turned into an obligation. This wiki records the risk.

**Paid care and seva.** Can a paid nurse be doing seva? The tradition places seva in the attitude of the one who serves. A paid carer can serve with full attention, and an unpaid daughter can serve with resentment.

**What the research shows.** A 2013 systematic review found that volunteering was linked with lower depression and better wellbeing, while noting that evidence from controlled trials was weaker.[^4] The tradition's claim is older and different in kind, and the research only touches its edge.

## Why it matters

**It is the householder's most available practice.** Caring for children, for an ageing parent, for a neighbour, is already happening. Seva asks only that it be done with attention.

**It is often the way back from isolation.** People who have withdrawn from the world frequently return through doing something for someone else, with their hands, before they are ready to talk.

**It loosens the grip of the self.** Time spent attending to someone else is time in which [[Ahamkara|ahamkara]], the I-maker, has less to do.
