# Dharma

## What it is

Dharma is what holds a thing in its place and keeps it being what it is. Applied to a person it is the conduct proper to them. Applied to a society it is the order that keeps it from coming apart. Applied to anything at all it is that thing's own nature, the way heat is the dharma of fire.

It is the first of the four *purusharthas*, the aims of a human life, and it comes before *artha*, wealth, and *[[Kama|kama]]*, desire. The sequence is the teaching. Wealth and desire are both legitimate and both placed second and third, which is to say that the boundary is meant to be settled before the pursuit begins rather than repaired afterwards.

The single most common error in English is to translate dharma as religion. It is not a body of belief and has no creed to assent to. A person is not asked what they hold true. They are asked what holds together because of how they act.

## Sanskrit and etymology

*Dharma* (धर्म) comes from the root *dhṛ*, to hold, to bear, to support, to uphold.[^1] Everything the word does follows from that root.

The *Mahabharata* makes the etymology carry the definition. In the Karna Parva, Krishna explains the term by pointing at the root itself: it is called dharma because it upholds, and what upholds and sustains people is dharma.[^1] The argument is circular by design. The text is refusing to define dharma by content, and defining it instead by function. Whatever holds a thing together is that thing's dharma, and you find out what it is by seeing what happens when it is absent.

The older Vedic word in this territory is *ṛta*, the cosmic order that keeps the seasons and the stars regular. Dharma is the later term, and the shift from one to the other is a shift from an order simply observed in the world to an order a person is responsible for maintaining.

## The ten characteristics

The *Manusmriti* gives the most commonly cited list of what dharma looks like in a person, in ten items.[^2]

| Sanskrit | Sense |
|---|---|
| *dhṛti* | steadiness, perseverance |
| *kṣamā* | forbearance, forgiveness |
| *dama* | self-restraint |
| *asteya* | not taking what is not given |
| *śauca* | cleanliness, inner and outer |
| *indriya-nigraha* | governance of the senses |
| *dhī* | intelligence, discernment |
| *vidyā* | learning, accurate knowledge |
| *satya* | truth |
| *akrodha* | absence of anger |

Two features of the list are worth noticing. Not one item is a belief. And the list is almost entirely composed of restraints and capacities rather than actions, which is consistent with a concept defined by what it holds rather than by what it does.

## The kinds of dharma

The literature distinguishes several senses, and most confusion about the word comes from collapsing them.

**Sadharana dharma** is the universal kind, binding on everyone regardless of position. The ten characteristics above belong here.

**Svadharma** is one's own, particular to a person's situation, stage of life and capacity. The Bhagavad Gita states in both its third and eighteenth chapters that one's own dharma performed imperfectly is better than another's performed well, which is among the most quoted and most misapplied sentences in the text. See Swadharma.

**Ashrama dharma** is what belongs to a stage of life, and it is the piece most relevant to a householder. The tradition has a full account of obligations proper to someone raising a family and running a home, which is why this work does not treat the householder as a lesser case.

**Apad dharma** is conduct permitted in extremity, when the ordinary rules would destroy the thing they exist to protect. Its presence in the literature is evidence that the system was never intended to be applied mechanically.

## What is contested

**Whether dharma is fixed or situational** is argued inside the source texts rather than only about them. The *Mahabharata*, which is the longest sustained treatment, repeatedly stages situations where two dharmas conflict and no clean answer exists, and states in its own voice that the truth of dharma is hidden in a cave. A tradition that wanted a rulebook would not have written that epic.

**Varna-based dharma carries the heaviest modern objection.** Duty assigned by birth is the aspect of the classical system that B. R. Ambedkar attacked directly in *Annihilation of Caste*, and the critique has not been answered by restating the older texts more sympathetically. This wiki's position is set out on [[Four Dharmas]] and is not repeated here.

**"Sanatana dharma" is a term with a history.** It appears in the older literature meaning something like the eternal or perennial order, and its use as the standard self-description of a religion is substantially a nineteenth and twentieth century development. See [[Sanatan Dharma]].

**The translation problem is not solved.** Duty, righteousness, law, virtue, order and religion have all been offered and each misses. Most careful writing now leaves the word untranslated, which is what this wiki does.

## Why it matters

**It is the first purushartha, and the order is the whole argument.** A great deal of what this work says about money rests on nothing more than that sequence. The essay [[No Bank Would Lend His Father Two Lakh Rupees]] is an extended demonstration of what happens when the earning is built first and the boundary is looked for afterwards, and [[Oh Money In This Too]] is the same claim made directly.

**It makes the commerce position coherent rather than defensive.** A tradition that placed wealth second would have no reason to be embarrassed by it and no reason to let it run first. That is a more stable footing than either the wellness industry's discomfort about charging or its enthusiasm for it.

**It gives the practice something to be for.** Steadiness, restraint, governance of the senses and absence of anger are four of the ten characteristics, and they are also, fairly precisely, what the morning practice and the evening review are working on. The [[Shadripus]] are the same territory from the other direction.

**It is what "the old way" means.** The phrase used across this work points at an order a person maintains by conduct, over a long time, in an ordinary house. Dharma is the word for that.
