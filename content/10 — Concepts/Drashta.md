# Drashta

## What it is

Drashta is the seer: the awareness that watches thoughts, feelings and events without being any of them. *Drashta bhava* is the attitude of the seer, the practice of standing in that place on purpose. A thought arises, and the practitioner notices that it has arisen. A feeling comes, and the practitioner notices the feeling and also the one who is noticing.

The *Yoga Sutras* treat this as the point of the whole discipline. When the movements of the mind are stilled, the seer rests in its own nature.[^1]

## Sanskrit and etymology

*Draṣṭṛ* (द्रष्टृ), in the nominative *draṣṭā*, comes from the root *dṛś*, to see. The same root gives *darśana*, a seeing, which is also the word for a school of philosophy and for the sight of a deity. *Bhāva* means attitude or state of being. The closely related word *sākṣin*, the witness, is used in Samkhya and Vedanta for the same idea.

## Where it appears in the canon

The *Yoga Sutras* give the seer its clearest definition. When the mind is still, the seer abides in itself. At other times it seems to take the form of whatever is moving through the mind.[^1] The seer is pure seeing, which looks out through the mind.[^2] The cause of suffering, the thing to be avoided, is the joining of the seer with what is seen.[^3] And the movements of the mind are always known to the seer, because the seer does not change.[^4]

The *Mundaka Upanishad* gives the most famous picture of it: two birds, close friends, sit on the same tree. One eats the sweet fruit. The other eats nothing, and watches.[^5]

The *Brihadaranyaka Upanishad* calls it the unseen seer, the unheard hearer, the unthought thinker.[^6]

In this lineage the seer is trained directly. In *antar mouna*, inner silence, the first stage is simply to watch the senses and then the thoughts, with the instruction to remain the witness. In [[Yoga Nidra]] the practitioner is asked again and again to be aware that they are aware.[^7]

## What is contested

**One seer or many.** [[Samkhya]], the philosophy beneath the *Yoga Sutras*, holds that there are many seers, one for each being. Advaita Vedanta holds that the witness in every being is the same one. The difference matters philosophically. In practice the instruction is the same: step back and watch.

**Witness or withdrawal.** A clinician would point out that stepping back from experience can turn into detachment from it, a numbness that looks like calm. The tradition draws the same line. The seer is described as fully present and unaffected. A practitioner who feels less, cares less or remembers less has slipped into [[Tamas|tamas]], dullness, which can feel similar from the inside. Psychology has its own name for the healthy version, decentering: seeing a thought as a thought.[^8]

## Why it matters

Most suffering in a householder's day comes from being swept away: a remark becomes an argument, a worry becomes a sleepless night. Drashta bhava puts a small space between the event and the reaction. A few minutes of watching each morning makes that space easier to find in the middle of the day. Over years, the tradition says, the space widens until the seer is recognised as what a person always was.
