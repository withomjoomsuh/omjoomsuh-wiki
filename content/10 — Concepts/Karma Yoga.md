# Karma Yoga

## What it is

Karma yoga is the discipline of acting without holding on to what the action will produce. It is one of the paths the Bhagavad Gita sets out, and the one addressed to a person who cannot withdraw from the world and is not being asked to.

The claim underneath it is blunt. Action is unavoidable. The Gita's third chapter argues that nobody can remain even momentarily without acting, since the constituents of nature compel activity regardless of intention, and that sitting still while the mind runs on the objects of sense is worse than acting, because it adds pretence to the compulsion. If action cannot be escaped, then the only available question is how it is held.

Karma yoga answers that the binding element is the attachment to the fruit rather than the doing. Remove the grip on the outcome and the same action stops accumulating consequence for the one performing it. The action continues. The ownership of it, which is the work of [[Ahamkara|ahamkara]], is what is set down.

## Sanskrit and etymology

*Karma* (कर्म) is from the root *kṛ*, to do or make. It means the act, the work, and also the residue an act leaves. The word carries both the deed and its long consequence, which is why the same term does duty for "chore" and for a theory of moral causation.

*Yoga* (योग) is from *yuj*, to yoke or join. The compound therefore reads as the yoking of action, or the joining accomplished by means of action.

*Niṣkāma karma* (निष्काम कर्म), the phrase most used for the practice, is *niṣ*, without, joined to *[[Kama|kāma]]*, desire. Desireless action. The term names the absence of a motive rather than the presence of a technique, and the practice is usually described in those negative terms throughout the literature.[^1]

## Where it appears in the canon

**Bhagavad Gita 2.47** is the verse the whole discipline is built on. It states that a person's claim extends to action alone and never to its fruits, that the fruits should not be the motive of action, and that attachment to inaction is equally to be avoided.[^2] The third clause is the one commonly dropped. The verse forecloses withdrawal as thoroughly as it forecloses grasping.

**Chapter three**, titled *Karma Yoga*, develops the argument at length. Its central move is to reframe ordinary work as *yajna*, offering. Work performed as an offering rather than an acquisition does not bind, and the chapter builds a picture of a world sustained by a cycle of such offerings, in which a person who consumes without contributing is described as living by theft.

The chapter also introduces *lokasangraha*, the holding-together of the world, as a motive that remains available to someone who has no personal stake left. It is the Gita's answer to the obvious objection: if you want nothing, why act at all.

**Chapter eighteen** returns to the theme and distinguishes *sannyasa*, the renunciation of actions, from *tyaga*, the renunciation of their fruits, and endorses the second. It also gives the verse on *svadharma*, holding that one's own duty performed imperfectly is better than another's performed well.

**Patanjali's Yoga Sutras** use a similar-sounding term for something different. *Kriya yoga*, defined at the opening of the second chapter as austerity, self-study and surrender, is a preparatory discipline and should not be conflated with the Gita's karma yoga.

**Swami Vivekananda's *Karma-Yoga***, delivered as lectures in New York and published in 1896, is the text that established karma yoga in English as a freestanding path rather than a chapter heading, and much of the modern usage descends from it.

**In the [[Bihar School of Yoga]]**, karma yoga is taught in the most ordinary possible form: assigned work in the ashram, done attentively, with no distinction drawn between the dignity of one task and another. The teaching is carried by the allocation rather than the explanation.

## What is contested

**Whether the Gita's position is activist or quietist** has been argued for more than a century. Bal Gangadhar Tilak's *Gita Rahasya* read the text as a call to engaged action in the world, against a commentarial tradition that had leaned towards renunciation. Gandhi's reading, published as *Anasakti Yoga*, kept the activism and bound it to non-attachment and to non-violence. Both are serious readings of the same chapters.

**Whether desireless action is psychologically possible** is the practitioner's objection and it is a fair one. A person who works while genuinely indifferent to the result tends to work badly. The usual response inside the tradition is that the discipline concerns attachment rather than care, and that the distinction is learned by practice instead of settled by argument. This wiki records the objection as unresolved.

**Whether karma yoga is a complete path or a preparation** divides the commentators. Shankara treats it largely as purification preceding knowledge. Ramanuja and others treat it as sufficient in itself.

## Why it matters

**It is the form of practice available to a householder.** Nearly everyone this work addresses is carrying a job, a household, or both, and cannot add hours. Karma yoga is the one discipline that requires no additional time, because its object is the work already being done.

**It is the reason the teaching does not ask anyone to leave.** A tradition that located the spiritual life exclusively in withdrawal would have nothing to say to a woman running a home. The Gita's position is that the field is wherever a person already stands.

**It reframes the invisible work.** The domestic labour that fills most of the day for most of the people this work reaches is, on this account, not a lesser category of activity waiting for real practice to begin. Whether that reframing consoles or conceals is worth asking honestly, since the same argument has been used to make unrewarded labour easier to extract.

**It is the ground under the commerce position.** The [[Four Dharmas|dharmic]] framing of exchange that this work uses, where a transaction is an offering rather than an extraction, is chapter three of the Gita applied to a business.
