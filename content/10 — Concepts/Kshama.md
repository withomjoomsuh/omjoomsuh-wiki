# Kshama

## What it is

Kshama is forgiveness and forbearance: the strength to bear a wrong without returning it. The *Manusmriti* lists it second among the ten marks of [[Dharma]], straight after steadiness.[^1] The *Bhagavad Gita* lists it among the qualities of a person born to a divine nature.[^2]

Forgiveness in this sense is an act of strength. It is chosen by someone who could strike back and does not.

## Sanskrit and etymology

*Kṣamā* (क्षमा) comes from the root *kṣam*, to bear, to endure, to be patient. *Kshama* is also an old name for the earth, which carries everything that is put on it. The image is useful. The earth does not pretend nothing was dropped on it. It holds the weight.

## Where it appears in the canon

The *Manusmriti* names the ten marks of dharma: steadiness, forgiveness, self-restraint, not stealing, cleanliness, governance of the senses, intelligence, learning, truth and freedom from anger.[^1]

In the *Gita*, Krishna names kshama twice. Once among the divine qualities, beside vigour and steadiness.[^2] Once among the qualities in which he himself is present.[^3]

The *Mahabharata* gives forgiveness its fullest argument. In the forest, Draupadi tells Yudhishthira that a man who always forgives is despised by his servants and his enemies alike, and quotes Prahlada's counsel that neither force nor forgiveness is right at every time. Yudhishthira answers with a long praise of forgiveness as the thing that holds the world together.[^4] The text lets both speeches stand.

## What is contested

**When forgiveness is strength and when it is surrender.** The *Mahabharata* stages exactly this argument and does not settle it. Draupadi's point is that forgiveness given at the wrong time invites more harm. Yudhishthira's point is that without forgiveness there is no end to harm.

**Forgiving and suppressing.** Modern psychology draws a line the tradition would recognise. Pushing an emotion down without working it through, which researchers call suppression, is linked to worse wellbeing and closeness than dealing with it openly.[^5] On the yogic view the swallowed anger does not disappear. It becomes a [[Samskaras|samskara]]. Kshama in the full sense comes after the anger has been seen and felt. It does not skip that step.

## Why it matters

Every family runs on forgiveness, and most of it goes unspoken. The tradition's contribution is to say that forgiveness is a discipline with a place in the order of dharma, and that it can be practised. The practice starts with noticing anger, *krodha*, one of the [[Shadripus]], before it is acted on, and with the plain admission of what was done. [[Mouna]], silence held with awareness, can give the anger room to be seen without being fed.
