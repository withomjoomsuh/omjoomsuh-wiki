# Satya

## What it is

Satya is truthfulness, the second of the five yamas, the restraints Patanjali places at the start of the path. It asks more than the absence of lies. It asks that what a person thinks, says and does line up.

## Sanskrit and etymology

*Satya* (सत्य) comes from *sat*, that which is. Truth, in this sense, is what is real. A truthful person is one whose words match what is.

## Where it appears in the canon

**Yoga Sutras 2.30** lists the five yamas: non-harming, truthfulness, non-stealing, restraint of the senses and non-grasping. **2.36** says that when truthfulness is established, a person's words come to bear fruit.[^1] Vyasa's commentary adds that speech which harms is not satya even when it is accurate, which places truthfulness under non-harming.

**Bhagavad Gita 17.15** describes the austerity of speech as words that cause no distress, and that are true, pleasant and beneficial.[^2]

**Manusmriti 4.138** gives the household version: speak the truth, speak what is pleasant, do not speak an unpleasant truth, and do not speak a pleasant falsehood.[^3]

## What is contested

**Truth against kindness.** The Manusmriti line can be read as permission to keep a hard truth back for the sake of peace. Japanese life has its own words for the same tension, *tatemae* and *honne*, the face shown and the feeling kept, and the psychiatrist Takeo Doi wrote at length about how the two are held together.[^4] Vyasa settles the matter by ranking non-harming first. Many practitioners find that settlement too easy, because a truth withheld for years can harm the one who withholds it. This wiki records the tension as live.

## Why it matters

**It starts the practice in ordinary speech.** The yamas come before posture and breath. A householder can practise satya at the dinner table today.

**The gap costs energy.** Saying one thing and meaning another is tiring, and the tiredness accumulates. Many people only notice how much the gap weighed when it closes.

**It gives words their weight.** Sutra 2.36 suggests that the words of a person who does not lie come to carry force. The everyday version is familiar: people listen to someone whose yes means yes.
