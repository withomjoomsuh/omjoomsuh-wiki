# Yoga Sutras

## What it is

The *Yoga Sutras* are a Sanskrit text of about 195 short aphorisms, attributed to [[Patanjali]], that set out yoga as a discipline of the mind. Each sutra is a thread, often only a few words long, meant to be memorised and then unpacked by a teacher or a commentary. Read without a commentary the text is almost a list of headings. Read with one, it is a complete map of the inner life as classical yoga understood it.

## Sanskrit and etymology

*Sūtra* (सूत्र) means a thread, and by extension a short rule that holds a larger teaching together. The same root gives the English word *suture*. A sutra text is written to be compact enough to carry in memory.

## The four chapters

**Samadhi Pada**, on absorption, about 51 sutras. It opens with the definition: yoga is the stilling of the movements of the mind, *yogaś citta-vṛtti-nirodhaḥ*.[^1] It names the five kinds of mental movement that [[Chitta]] covers and the paths of practice and detachment.

**Sadhana Pada**, on practice, about 55 sutras. It gives *kriya yoga*, the yoga of action, as discipline, self-study and surrender. It names the five [[Kleshas|kleshas]], the roots of suffering, and sets out the eight limbs of practice: the yamas, the niyamas, posture, breath control, [[Pratyahara|pratyahara]], concentration, meditation and absorption.[^2] Its definition of posture is two words, steady and easy, *sthira sukham*.[^3]

**Vibhuti Pada**, on powers, about 55 sutras. It covers the last three limbs and the subtle capacities said to arise from them, with a warning that they are obstacles if clung to.

**Kaivalya Pada**, on freedom, 34 sutras. It ends with *kaivalya*, when the seer rests in its own nature. See [[Moksha]].[^4]

## The commentaries

The text has always travelled with commentary. The earliest, attributed to Vyasa, is so close to the sutras that some scholars treat the two as one work. Later commentaries include those of Vachaspati Mishra, King Bhoja and Vijnanabhikshu. In this lineage the main reference is Swami Satyananda Saraswati's *Four Chapters on Freedom*.[^5]

## What is contested

**Date and author.** Estimates run from the second century BCE to the fifth century CE. See [[Patanjali]].

**Number of sutras.** Editions count 195 or 196, depending on how one sutra in the third chapter is divided.

**Its place in history.** The text was widely commented on for centuries, then fell into relative neglect, and was revived in the late nineteenth century, largely through Swami Vivekananda's *Raja Yoga* (1896). David Gordon White's history of the text traces that rise, eclipse and return.[^6] Today it is often treated as the founding text of all yoga. Hatha yoga, tantra and the devotional paths have their own sources, and the Sutras say very little about posture.

## Why it matters

For a practitioner, the *Yoga Sutras* give the reason for every practice. Posture steadies the body so the breath can settle. The breath settles so the senses can turn inward. The senses turn inward so the mind can be watched, and its movements slowly stilled. A householder does not need to master all four chapters. The second sutra alone, and the eight limbs, are enough to organise a life of practice.
