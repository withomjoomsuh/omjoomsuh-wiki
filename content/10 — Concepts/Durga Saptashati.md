# Durga Saptashati

## What it is

The *Durga Saptashati* is a hymn to the Goddess in seven hundred verses. It is also called the *Devi Mahatmya*, the Glory of the Goddess, and in many homes simply the *Chandi Path*. It forms thirteen chapters of the *Markandeya Purana* and is one of the foundational texts of the Shakta tradition, the stream of Hindu practice that worships the divine as feminine, as [[Shakti]].[^1]

It is recited above all during Navaratri, the nine nights of the Goddess in spring and autumn, and in many households on any day when a difficulty needs to be crossed.

## Sanskrit and etymology

*Saptaśatī* (सप्तशती) means "the seven hundred", after its number of verses. *Durgā* is read in the tradition as the one who is difficult to approach, and also as the one who carries a person across what is difficult to cross. *Devī Māhātmya* means the greatness of the Goddess.

## The three stories

The text tells three battles, each won by a different form of the Goddess.

**The first episode**, chapter 1. Vishnu lies in yogic sleep while two demons, Madhu and Kaitabha, threaten Brahma. The Goddess as Mahakali, the power of that sleep, withdraws from Vishnu so he can wake and destroy them.

**The middle episode**, chapters 2 to 4. The buffalo demon Mahishasura defeats the gods. Their combined radiance takes the form of the Goddess as Mahalakshmi, who kills him.

**The final episode**, chapters 5 to 13. The demon brothers Shumbha and Nishumbha seek to possess the Goddess. As Mahasaraswati she destroys them and their generals, including Chanda, Munda and Raktabija, whose every drop of blood spawns a new demon.

## How it is read in this lineage

The Shakta tradition reads the demons as forces inside a person. Raktabija, who multiplies with every wound, is the obsessive thought that grows each time it is fought head-on. Mahishasura is brute inertia, close to [[Tamas|tamas]]. The [[Shadripus]] page follows this reading.

## What is contested

**Date.** Thomas Coburn's study places the text around the fifth or sixth century CE, as the moment the Goddess tradition crystallised into one figure.[^2]

**What belongs to the seven hundred.** Recitation traditionally includes hymns before and after the main text, such as the *Kavacha*, *Argala* and *Kilaka*. The [[32 Names of Durga]] are recited in the same tradition and printed with it in many editions, as an attached hymn outside the count of seven hundred.
