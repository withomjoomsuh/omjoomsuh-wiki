# Ahamkara

## What it is

Ahamkara is the function in a person that takes an experience and claims it. It is the I-maker: the operation by which seeing becomes *my* seeing, a pain becomes *my* pain, and a set of qualities becomes *who I am*.

In [[Samkhya]] it is the fourth of the twenty-five tattvas and the second evolute of Prakriti, arising out of *mahat* or [[Buddhi]] and giving rise in turn to [[Manas]], the ten faculties of sense and action, and the five subtle essences. It sits in the middle of the sequence, which is the structurally important fact about it. Intellect comes before it. The senses come after it. A person is therefore not a self that subsequently acquires experiences; the sense of being a separate self shows up partway through the unfolding, with discrimination already operating above it.

The word is often translated as ego, and the translation costs more than it gives. Ego in English carries a moral charge, something a person has too much of. Ahamkara is a function rather than a fault. It is doing its work equally in arrogance and in ordinary competence, and a person with none of it would be unable to act at all.

## Sanskrit and etymology

*Ahaṃkāra* (अहंकार) is *aham*, "I", joined to *kāra*, "maker" or "doer", from the root *kṛ*, to do or make. The compound is literally the I-maker, and Sanskrit forms it as an ongoing activity rather than a thing. The tradition is describing a verb that English keeps turning into a noun.

The *Samkhya Karika* defines its function in one word: *abhimāna*.[^1] Usually rendered as self-assertion, self-conceit, or the act of regarding something as belonging to oneself. *Abhimāna* is the appropriating movement, the claim of ownership over what is passing through.

## The threefold division

Ahamkara is not uniform. Depending on which of the three [[Guna|gunas]] predominates in it, it operates in three modes, and each mode produces a different part of the person.[^2]

| Mode | Guna | What comes from it |
|---|---|---|
| **Vaikarika** or *sattvika* | [[Sattva]] | The eleven faculties: [[Manas]], the five senses of knowing, the five of action |
| **Taijasa** | [[Rajas]] | No product of its own; supplies the energy for both other modes |
| **Bhutadi** | [[Tamas]] | The five *tanmatras*, the subtle essences, from which the gross elements follow |

The middle row is the one worth pausing on. *Taijasa*, the rajasic mode, produces nothing by itself. It is the power that enables the other two to produce anything at all. The system is saying that the ego's energy and the ego's outputs are separate things, and that the same drive underwrites both the clarity of the senses and the density of the material world.

The practical consequence for anyone working on themselves: you cannot remove ahamkara without removing the faculties. The eleven instruments a person perceives and acts with are *products* of it. This is why the tradition speaks of seeing through it rather than destroying it.

## Where it appears in the canon

**The *Samkhya Karika*** gives the definition in verse 24 and the derivation of the eleven faculties and five tanmatras from the three modes.[^1] Everything above that is described as classical comes from there.

**Patanjali's Yoga Sutras** approach the same territory through a different word. *Asmita*, I-am-ness, is listed as the second of the five *kleshas*, the afflictions, and is defined as the apparent identity of the power of seeing with the power of the instrument that sees. Yoga treats the mistake as a thing to be dismantled by practice. Samkhya treats it as a structural feature to be seen through by discrimination. Same phenomenon, two methods.

**The Bhagavad Gita** uses the term in its ordinary moral sense as well as its technical one, listing freedom from ahamkara among the marks of a person who has arrived somewhere, and naming it among the constituents of the field in its analysis of field and knower.

**In the Puranic and Vedantic literature** the threefold division reappears with the same three names, which is part of why the scheme is worth learning once: it recurs across texts that otherwise disagree.

## What is contested

**Whether "ego" is ever an acceptable translation.** The technical objection is that it imports a Freudian structure with no counterpart here. The practical objection runs the other way: no better single English word exists, and refusing all translation makes the concept inaccessible. This wiki uses the Sanskrit and explains it.

**Whether ahamkara is one function or several.** Later commentators differ on whether the three modes are aspects of a single tattva or effectively three, which matters for the enumeration that gives Samkhya its name.

**Where it sits relative to [[Buddhi]].** The classical order puts buddhi above ahamkara, and several later traditions reverse or blur this. The order carries the argument, so the disagreement is not cosmetic.

## Why it matters

**It is the mechanism behind the [[Shadripus]].** The six inner enemies are described on that page as arising when buddhi is overwhelmed and conditioned impressions take the wheel. Ahamkara is what does the taking. Pride, envy and anger are all ownership operations: something has been claimed as mine, and the claim is now being defended.

**It explains what the evening review is doing.** Looking back at a day and noticing what stirred anger or envy is an exercise in watching the appropriating movement after the fact, when it is no longer live enough to defend itself. The practice is not asking a person to stop having an ego. It is asking them to see it work.

**It sets the tone of the teaching.** If the I-maker is a function rather than a flaw, then the work has no place for shame about having one. This matters for a reader who arrives at contemplative material expecting to be told that their sense of self is the problem. On this account it is the equipment.

**It is why "know yourself" is the wrong instruction in this tradition.** The instruction is closer to noticing which of the things you call yourself are being made, moment by moment, by a function that is itself part of nature. That is a different and more workable request.
