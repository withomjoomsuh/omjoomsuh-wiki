# Artha

## What it is

Artha is wealth and the means of a life: money, property, a livelihood, the power to provide for the people who depend on you. It is the second of the four aims of a human life, the [[Purusharthas|purusharthas]]. [[Dharma]] comes before it. [[Kama]] and [[Moksha]] come after.

The tradition does not treat wealth as a necessary evil. A householder is expected to earn, to keep and to give. The condition is that the earning happens inside dharma. Placing dharma first is the teaching: the boundary is set before the pursuit begins.

## Sanskrit and etymology

*Artha* (अर्थ) means aim, object or purpose. The same word also means the meaning of a sentence. The sense runs from "the thing a person seeks" to "the means a person lives by", and the double meaning is worth keeping. Wealth is what you aim at, and it is also what your aim means in practice.

## Where it appears in the canon

Kautilya's *Arthashastra*, the classical treatise on statecraft, states that artha is the chief of the three worldly aims, because dharma and kama both rest on it.[^1] A kingdom that cannot feed itself keeps no dharma.

The *Manusmriti* records the argument without hiding it. Some say the good is dharma and artha, some say kama and artha, some say dharma alone, some say artha alone. Its own settled view is that the good is the three together.[^2]

The *Bhagavad Gita* describes the person of a demonic nature as one who piles up wealth by unjust means in order to feed desire.[^3] The warning is aimed at the method of gaining wealth, and at the purpose it is put to.

The *Kama Sutra* opens by placing its subject inside the same three aims and says that a well-lived life gives each its proper time.[^4]

## What is contested

**Which comes first.** Kautilya puts artha first for a practical reason. The dharma texts put dharma first for a moral one. Both positions sit inside the tradition, and the *Manusmriti*'s compromise, the three together, is itself an admission that the question was argued.

**Where earning ends and greed begins.** The tradition names greed, *lobha*, as one of the six enemies of the mind, the [[Shadripus]]. It gives no figure at which wealth turns into greed. The test it offers is how the money was gained and what it is used for.

## Why it matters

For a householder, artha is daily. Rent, school fees, a parent's care, a business that has to survive a bad year. A teaching that ignored money would have nothing to say to most of a working life. The tradition's answer is to keep wealth in its place in the sequence: earned honestly, held without clinging, and given in part. Giving is where artha meets [[Seva]].
