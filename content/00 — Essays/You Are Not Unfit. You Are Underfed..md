# You Are Not Unfit. You Are Underfed.

*The gym builds one of your five bodies and starves the other four.*

---

I watch her finish her last set in the half-light, and I know the look before she does.

Six forty-five in the morning. The gym on the second floor of a Gurugram tower hums with that cold blue light, the kind that comes from tubes and not from the sun. A dumbbell goes back on the rack with a small iron clink, metal kissing metal. She straightens, breathing hard, and catches her own face in the long mirror. For half a second she looks at herself the way you look at a stranger on a train. Stronger arms than last year. A flatter stomach. And under all of it, plain as a watermark held to the light, something I have learned to read across a hundred such faces: she is tired in a place the workout cannot reach.

She has done everything right. That is the part no one will say to her.

She joined the gym. She hired the trainer. She learned to deadlift, to count protein in grams, to be awake and disciplined while the house slept. By every measure the fitness world gave her, she has passed. And still she is leaner and more tired. Clear-eyed at seven in the morning. Coming apart by nine at night. She has begun to carry a quiet, unspeakable suspicion: that she followed the instructions correctly and still arrived somewhere she never meant to go.

Here is what I would tell her. And I would tell it first, because the truth should not wait at the back of the room.

She is not unfit. She is underfed.

Not in the body the gym can see. That one is well served. Underfed in the four bodies it cannot.

The gym builds one of your five bodies and starves the other four.

This is not my idea. I am only a man who has been handed something old and is trying to live inside it. The teaching is far older than the city she trains in. The rishis who composed the *Taittiriya Upanishad*, in the section called the *Brahmananda Valli*, described a human being not as one body but as five sheaths, set one inside the other like the chambers of a flute. The [[Pancha Kosha|pancha kosha]]. The [[Bihar School of Yoga]], the [[Saraswati Order|Saraswati Sampradaya]] carried forward by [[Swami Satyananda Saraswati|Swami Satyananda]] and [[Swami Niranjanananda Saraswati|Swami Niranjanananda]], has kept this map alive and practical into our own loud time. It is Vedantic teaching, Upanishadic at its root. I want to name its lineage with care, because a thing this old deserves to be named correctly and not blurred into whatever stands nearby.

The five, named once, plainly.

*Annamaya kosha* — the food sheath. The flesh, the muscle and bone made of what you eat. This is the body the gym serves, and serves well.

*Pranamaya kosha* — the breath body. The sheath of [[Prana|prana]], the energy that moves the blood and the lungs and the slow tide of the nervous system.

*Manomaya kosha* — the mind body. The sheath of thought and feeling, the changing weather of the moods.

*Vijnanamaya kosha* — the body of discernment. The witness. The intelligence that stands back from the noise and chooses.

*Anandamaya kosha* — the innermost sheath. The ground beneath all the others. The still place from which happiness rises for no reason at all.

The gym lives entirely in the first. It is excellent there. It is simply not the whole house. And one lit room does not warm the others.

Let me show you the other four the way they actually arrive. Not as doctrine. As her ordinary Tuesday.

The starved breath body is the exhale that never finishes. Watch her breathe at her desk at eleven in the morning. Short sips at the top of the lungs. Air pulled in and barely let go. A breath shaped like a person bracing for a knock at the door. She can squat her own bodyweight. She cannot empty her lungs to the floor of them. The strongest part of breathing is the surrender at the end of the out-breath. That is the part the day has trained her to skip.

The starved mind body is nine o'clock at night. All day she held. The deadlines, the children's homework, the household's small wars — held, held, held, the way you carry a full glass across a crowded room. Then the help stacks a plate wrong, or the milk has turned, and something huge and out of all proportion comes up out of her over a thing the size of a teaspoon. She hears her own voice climb and does not know it. That is not a flaw in her character. That is a sheath running on fumes, with nothing left in the tank by the time the sun goes down.

The starved body of discernment is the right things done in the wrong order. She performs all the correct acts — the training, the supplements, the eight thousand steps. But no one sits quietly at the centre arranging them. No witness decides what this body needs today, as against what the internet decided in general, last year, for everyone. Strength without discernment is a fast car with no one at the wheel. The acts are right. The one who should choose among them has not been fed enough to sit up.

And the starved ground — the anandamaya — is the strangest hunger of the four. From the outside it wears the face of success. It is strength with no one home inside it. A body made handsome and capable, walking steadily through its own life, and somewhere beneath the floorboards a cold draught coming up through the place where contentment should sit. She built the whole house and forgot to light the lamp in the deepest room. Now she cannot say why a finished house feels so empty at dusk.

Here I am careful, because this is where people inflate things, and I will not.

There is an honest place where the old map and the new science touch. It is worth naming at its true size, and no wider. The breath body is not a poetry. The long, slow exhale — the part she keeps skipping — is the body's own brake. Draw the out-breath longer than the breath drawn in, and you lift what physiology calls vagal tone: the quiet work of the [[Vagus Nerve|nerve]] that tells the heart it is safe now to slow.

And now I will tell you the thing this whole letter was written to reach. Because the breath body is not fed by reading about it. It is fed by an act, repeated, in the dark.

Every weekday morning, before the city wakes, a small group of us sits down to chant. Nothing hurried. We take the [[Mahamrityunjaya]] mantra eleven times, each repetition carried out on a single breath. Then the [[Gayatri]], eleven times, each on a single breath. Then the [[32 Names of Durga|thirty-two names]] of the Goddess, three times over. Every repetition rides out on one long exhale, and the out-breath runs to twice the length of the breath we draw in. The chanting alone takes the better part of a quarter of an hour, and the clock is not the point. The breath is the point.

Watch what the breath does when it is shaped this way. On the long mantras a single cycle stretches until it lasts near nineteen seconds — close to six seconds drawing in, near thirteen seconds pouring out. Three breaths to a minute. On the Goddess's names the rhythm quickens to about five and a half breaths a minute, the out-breath near seven seconds long. We do not chase these numbers. They are simply what the mantra makes of the breath when you refuse to break it.

Here the doorway opens from the other end. In 2001 the *BMJ* published a study by Bernardi and his colleagues. They sat people down with the Latin rosary and with a Sanskrit yoga mantra, and they watched the breath find its own floor. Both, left to their own rhythm, slowed the breath to almost exactly six times a minute — and at that rate the heart and the breath fell into step, and the baroreflex, a core sign of a steady vagal system, rose. Six breaths a minute is the rate at which the body seems to answer. The thirty-two names sit almost exactly on it. The long mantras go slower still, down past that floor, into the deep country of the very long exhale. The rishis did not have the word vagal or the word baroreflex. They had the instruction, kept for six thousand years: chant it on one breath, let the out-breath be long. Two travellers, the same doorway, opposite ends of time.

I want to be plain about what this is and is not. The first of those mantras carries in its very name the conquering of death. I will not tell you it conquers any illness, or buys you a single extra day. I do not know that, and neither does anyone who would sell it to you. What I can tell you, with the science standing beside the tradition and neither leaning on the other, is what a long exhale, kept daily, does to the body that breathes it. It feeds vagal tone. It steadies the inflammatory weather. It builds back the resilience she runs dry of by nine at night. That is true. It is not a medicine. I will not dress it as one.

And the breath is only the half of this that instruments can weigh. We can chant only on the way out — so every mantra rides the very exhale that is calming the body. But what rides it is not empty sound. It is meaning, six thousand years old, laid onto the one breath in the day when the nervous system has gone quiet enough to receive it. The breath you can prove on a chart. What the words do to the mind, settling on a body already stilled, is the older story, and the chart keeps no column for it. The two together do something to a person that neither does alone. That hour is also the day's first hinge — the [[Sandhya|sandhya]], the dawn turning the tradition built into the day exactly where the body's own tide is meant to lift.

This is the difference the gym was never built to know. The gym trains hypertrophy — the muscle made larger. The tradition tends to something the old texts call *pushti* — strength that nourishes, strength with sap still in it, a fullness with someone living inside. A bigger body and a fed body are not the same body. She has been sold the first. She is quietly starving for the second. And what feeds the second is not a new lifestyle bolted onto the old one. It is a slower thing. A daily [[Sadhana|sadhana]]. A different life behaviour underneath the visible one.

So let me carry her back to where we began. The answer does not live in some new place. It lives in the same gym, the same mirror, the same body — with one thing in her hands she did not have when she first walked in.

Six forty-five. The cold blue light. The iron clink of the dumbbell going home on the rack. The same woman. The same strong arms. The same faint watermark of tiredness underneath. But now the tiredness has a name. It is not failure and it is not laziness. It is four hungers the room was never built to feed. The strength is real. She earned it, drop by drop, and no one should take it from her. It is simply one sheath, well fed, standing in a house of five.

What waits at ten past six is small, and I will keep it small, because nothing here is sold by the kilo and nothing here is quick. A little, daily. Drop by drop on parched earth. That is the only way water has ever reached anything dry. Before the body is made any stronger, the breath body is fed first — eleven breaths, then eleven more, each one long, the exhale poured out to the bottom, the mind riding down on the sound. We sit together for this at the nurturing centre each morning. Not because the hour holds any magic, but because the day has not yet reached you then, and you can give the first of yourself back to yourself. There is no finish line in it. There is no after-photograph at the end. There is only the oldest and least fashionable thing I know: that the one true miracle in this whole loud world is a person learning to stand, fed in all five of her bodies, on her own two feet.

She racks the weight. She turns toward the stairs and the waiting day. And somewhere below the gym a kitchen window has been left open. The first thing the morning gives her is not a thought at all but a smell — wet earth where someone has watered a low row of tulsi, and under it, faint, the camphor sweetness of a lamp just lit in a dark room.
